# Loom: the method research

A research agent's report, 2026-11-18, on how to draw cloth being woven within the app's budget: how a historic draft becomes the cloth on screen, the real thing in numbers, which view and what motion, the shimmer the idea was flagged for (measured), how it would be drawn and what it costs (measured), the risks, and what to mock up. Nothing here is built. Peg Board, which this idea named as its model, was cut on 2026-11-14; its code is read here as reference only (git `51a8a7f`).

**How to read the marks.** **Checked** means read on the page whose URL follows (or in the source list at the end). **Unchecked** means no page was read that states it ("snippet" where only a search result's summary was seen). **Derived** is my arithmetic on the figures named; **measured** is the probe below, on this Mac (Apple M3 Max, on mains power, lid open); **app** means read in this repo's code or docs.

## Recommendation in ten lines
1. **The view:** the cloth flat on, the fell fixed near the top, the cloth going down the screen as it is woven (§3, view B). At least four fifths of the screen is woven cloth at every moment; one piece gives way to the next at the fell after a few picks of heading, and the seam passes down the screen. No rising fell over bare warp, no hold, no clear: that was Peg Board's empty sheet and is Etching's act.
2. **The engine:** one full-screen `SKShader` reading a drawdown baked once per cloth, a texel per crossing (which thread is on top, where its float ends, its colour, a random number), Peg Board's per-cell pattern; the place in the work kept by the wall clock as Etching's is. No simulation, no photo (§5).
3. **The drafts:** real ones, transcribed by hand from public-domain scans (Bronson 1817, Worst 1918, the 18th-century German books; Atwater 1928 for the US only until 2027) and kept as WIF files. Not handweaving.net: subscription only, no licence. Overshot first, then summer and winter (§1).
4. **The scale:** life size. Coverlets are about 30 ends an inch with a pattern pick and its tabby about two ends tall, which on a 14-inch screen (254 ppi) is 8 px an end and 16 px a pair: the screen shows 11.9 × 7.7 in of cloth, one to four repeats across (§2).
5. **Shimmer:** every end, every pick and every step of the cloth a whole number of device pixels. Measured, that gives zero crawl and zero pumping at 3–12 px; a slide by fractions of a pixel makes the whole cloth breathe by up to 14 levels of 255. At least 6 px an end, 8 standard; the preview rounds to its own whole pixels; the shader filters by its footprint as a safety net (§4).
6. **Cost, measured:** 0.28–0.30 ms of GPU at 3024 × 1964, CPU at SpriteKit's floor (0.34 ms), 0.94 ms of GPU at 6K. Peg Board's shader read 0.34 in the same run (§6).
7. **Pace:** a weaver lays a pattern pick and its tabby about every 5 s; the standard would be half that pace, the cloth moving down 1.6 px a second in whole pixels and a screen woven in about 20 minutes, with a Speed knob from a strip a day up to a weaver's pace (§3).
8. **Colours:** indigo (measured woad dyeings, sRGB 36, 52, 63 to 88, 117, 138) or madder (rust to brownish rose; alum on wool 173, 104, 52) on yellowed natural cotton; indigo and madder together in one coverlet was common, so offer it as a third, argued (§2).
9. **Battery:** a cap of 15 with the pick fading across, or `BatteryPaced` at 10 lifted while a pick crosses (§3).
10. **Worst risks:** shimmer where no rule reaches (scaled display modes, the preview's overshot rows); a cloth that reads as basketry (it needs a thread width for each set, wool's fuzz, and to be judged beside photos of real coverlets); and any drift toward building a picture and holding it (§7).

## 1. Drafts and drawdowns

**From a draft to a drawdown.** A loom-controlled draft is three lists: the **threading** (which shaft each warp end goes through), the **tie-up** (which shafts each treadle lifts) and the **treadling** (which treadle each pick is woven on). A **liftplan** is the tie-up and treadling folded into one: for each pick, the shafts lifted. Either way the cloth is a grid of crossings, ends across and picks up, and at each one the rule is a single test: on a rising-shed loom, end *i* is on top at pick *j* when the shaft it is threaded on is among those lifted for pick *j*; otherwise the weft is (derived from the definitions; the probe's `drawdown` does exactly this). A tie-up written for a sinking shed (counterbalance looms; many old American drafts) marks the shafts that go down, so it is read inverted. The drawdown repeats with the threading across and the treadling up, so one repeat of each is all that has to be stored.

**What is on top is not all a crossing needs.** To shade a thread the shader also wants to know whether the float it belongs to carries on into the next crossing along its own thread or dives under there. The probe bakes that in with the drawdown, as two bits per crossing (does the float carry on before, after), so one texture read gives a crossing everything: which thread is on top, its colour, where its float starts and ends, and a random number for that thread (measured: the bake is 2.5–12.5 ms for a whole screen of crossings in a plain Swift loop, and one repeat of 120 × 120 crossings would be well under a millisecond).

**Pattern and tabby picks.** Overshot and summer-and-winter are woven with two shuttles: a heavy pattern weft (wool, dyed) alternating with a fine tabby weft (the warp's own cotton or linen, plain weave on shafts 1–3 and 2–4) that holds the cloth together. In the drawdown each is a row; on screen they are not the same height. The probe draws a pattern pick and its tabby as one pair of rows, the pattern pick taking a set share of the pair (`u_split`: 0.62, then 0.75 after the first still showed the tabby too strong), at no extra cost: the pair is found by one division and the row inside it by one comparison. Overshot's pattern weft makes three textures across a figure: **solid** (it floats over a whole block), **halftone** (it crosses a neighbouring block over every other end) and **ground** (it goes under, and the tabby shows); the probe's tie-up gives exactly that (treadle for block A lifts shafts 3–4, so the pattern floats over the A ends; derived).

**The file: WIF, the Weaving Information File.** Version 1.1 is dated 20 April 1997 (1.0, March 1996, by Ravi Nielsen of Maple Hill Software with the makers of Fiberworks, Swiftweave and others); any program may use the format, though the spec's text itself may not be redistributed (checked, [WIF]). It is plain INI-style text: `[WEAVING]` (shafts, treadles, and `Rising Shed`, which when absent means a sinking shed), `[THREADING]` as `end=shaft[,shaft]` (an end can be on more than one shaft), `[TIEUP]` as `treadle=shafts`, `[TREADLING]` as `pick=treadle`, or `[LIFTPLAN]` as `pick=shafts lifted`; `[COLOR PALETTE]` and `[COLOR TABLE]` (`n=R,G,B` in a stated range), `[WARP COLORS]`/`[WEFT COLORS]` (a palette index per thread), and `[WARP]`/`[WEFT]` with the count, a default colour, units, spacing and thickness. Keys count from 1, sections may be in any order and sparse, missing lines take the defaults (checked, [WIF]). Quirks of real files, from an open-source reader's comments: thread counts larger than the threading, junk in the treadling, files with both a liftplan and a treadling (checked, [PYW]). The spec never says which side end 1 is on; old drafts read from the right (Atwater 1928, p. 117, checked [ATW]). A reader for the app is a page of Swift: parse the sections, invert a tie-up whose shed is not rising, take one shaft per end, drop what it can't use.

**Where real historic drafts come from.**
- **handweaving.net** (Kris Bruland, since 2004, from Ralph Griswold's scans) holds 77,023 drafts in 81 collections, among them Ziegler 1677 (396), Frickinger (120), Kirschbaum 1771 (245), Bronson 1817 (28), Landes c. 1800 (83, profiles only), Boscher 1819 (653), Woolhever 1821 (380), Arnold 1825 (306), Grimes 1831 (93) and Oelsner 1915 (2,258) (checked, [HWN-API]). **It can't be the source:** a WIF can be downloaded only by a paying subscriber, there is no licence or terms of use anywhere on the site, only "© 2004 – Kris Bruland", and its drafts are his corrected readings of the scans, with "other interpretations possible" (checked, [HWN-JS]). Shipping them would need his written permission (the researcher's inference). It is still the best place to find which old drafts are worth transcribing, and their repeat sizes.
- **Griswold's archive** (University of Arizona, online since 1999; 459 books, 9,122 PDFs) carries no blanket licence: each item is marked "copyright expired", "published without a copyright notice" or "by permission of the author" (checked, [GRI]). His *Collection of Old Weave Patterns* (13,000+, 2004) says "individual patterns may be used freely without permission or attribution", but not the collection or substantial parts of it (checked, [GRI-OPB]).
- **So: transcribe our own, from public-domain scans,** as Etching made its own routes from public-domain prints. The books, each checked on its archive.org record: **Bronson 1817**, *The Domestic Manufacturer's Assistant* (twills, bird's-eye, diapers, eight-shaft coverlets including overshot; public domain by age; [BRO]); **Worst 1918**, *Foot-Power Loom Weaving* (four-shaft overshot on grids, including Whig Rose, Snail's Trail and Cat's Paw, Governor's Garden, Chariot Wheel and Sea Star; twills, double weave; marked not in copyright, Worst died 1949; [WOR]); **Hall 1912**, *A Book of Hand-Woven Coverlets* (marked not in copyright; mostly photos and names, one page of old Kentucky drafts; [HAL]); **Hargrove 1792** (Public Domain Mark; contents not read; [HAR]); **Frickinger** 1740 and 1783, **Lumscher** 1720 and 1736, **Kirschbaum** 1771 (German block and damask patterns for 15–40 shafts, public domain by age; [GER]); and **Atwater 1928**, *The Shuttle-Craft Book* (overshot, summer and winter, double weave, twills; Whig Rose is her draft 91, Sunrise 102): public domain in the US since 1 January 2024, but she died in 1956, so under copyright in life-plus-70 countries until 1 January 2027 (checked, [ATW], [DUKE]). Use Atwater's drafts only for the US, or wait until 2027; the 1951 and later editions are not free. Angstadt's book exists only as a 1976 facsimile still in copyright (unchecked, snippet).
- **Repeat sizes found:** Bronson's No. 17 eight-shaft coverlet 72 ends, No. 21 Diamond Coverlet 104 (checked, [HWN-API]); Worst's Whig Rose about 170 squares across its two strips by eye (unchecked). No public-domain digital draft of Whig Rose, Lover's Knot, Blooming Leaf, Sunrise or Governor's Garden with a counted repeat was found: they would be counted off Worst's grid figures.

**How the kinds draw down** (checked, [ATW] pages as given, [WOR] p. 100): overshot's tabby is 1–3 against 2–4 and its pattern treadles are tied on adjacent pairs (1–2, 2–3, 3–4, 1–4), so neighbouring blocks overlap into halftones; a block where the pattern turns back has an odd number of ends to keep the tabby alternating; tabby picks alternate with pattern picks and treadlings leave them out (pp. 47, 112–113, 159). Woven as drawn in, a figure makes a true diagonal; "rose fashion" is another treadling of the same threading, and Whig Rose woven as drawn in becomes a form of Lover's Knot (pp. 117–118): so one warp gives several cloths, as §3 uses. The longest weft float in 175 of 199 four-shaft overshot drafts on handweaving.net is 2–7 ends, most often 5 (checked, [HWN-API]). Summer and winter ties the pattern weft down on every fourth end, floats of three at most, blocks any size (p. 207). Double weave needs eight shafts or more, four a block, the two cloths swapping faces (p. 213). Jacquard has no shafts: each hook lifts its own ends, so the design is the draft (checked, [JAC]).

**Which kinds grow into something worth watching at desktop scale.** Judged by what a pick adds, how big a figure is against the screen, and how much of the screen is figure rather than ground:
- **Overshot** (coverlets: Whig Rose, Sunrise, Lover's Knot and their kin): the strongest. Big figures (stars, roses, wheels, tables) in solid and halftone over a fine ground; each pick adds a visible strip of floats, and a figure takes tens of pattern picks to close, so the eye can watch it form. Its long floats are also the softest-looking texture. First choice.
- **Summer and winter:** the same block figures with floats of three at most, so the figure is crisper and the texture finer, nearer a mosaic. A good second kind.
- **Double weave:** two layers swapping, so blocks are solid indigo or solid natural on both faces with no floats at all: the most graphic, and the most like a pixel picture, which is where it brushes against Peg Board. Worth one mock-up, not the lead.
- **Twills** (straight, point, rose path, broken): fine all-over diagonals and diamonds. Each pick moves a whole-width line by one end, so as it grows it reads as texture, not as a figure. They are also the worst case for moiré (§4). Good as cloth between figured pieces, or as the plainest pick.
- **Jacquard coverlets** (figured, with borders, medallions, birds, the weaver's name and date in a corner): the most picture-like, but they have no shaft draft at all: each end is lifted on its own by punched cards, so the "draft" is the figure itself, a bitmap. The only route to real ones is to trace a public-domain photo of a coverlet into a grid, which is Etching's act (a picture built line by line) by another name, and a picture that ends. Leave for later.

**How many repeats fit.** A repeat of *n* ends at *P* device pixels an end is *nP* pixels wide: at 8 px (life size on a 14-inch screen, §2), a 3024 px screen holds 378 ends, so Bronson's 72-end coverlet five times across and his 104-end Diamond nearly four (derived). Up the screen, a pattern pick and its tabby take 2*P*, 16 px, so 1964 px is about 120 pattern picks (derived). Real repeats of 3.25–8.6 in come out one and a half to three and a half across at life size (§2), which is the right amount: a figure is big enough to watch close, and its neighbours show it is cloth and not a picture. The largest (Braddock's Defeat, 13 in) is wider than the screen, so a Thread size below life size is worth having too.

## 2. The real thing in numbers
Gathered by a second agent for this report and marked as it read them (source keys at the end). Smithsonian (NMAH) records were read through its Open Access API; Colonial Williamsburg, Vesterheim and Historic New England refused automated reading, so their figures are snippets only.

**Setts, ends × picks an inch.**
- **Overshot:** all-white cotton, New England 1811, 28 × 32 (11.0 × 12.6 a cm), "one pattern weft inserted between each ground weft", two panels woven as one length, 80 × 71.75 in (checked, [SI-1811]). "Braddock's Defeat", New York c. 1843: 2-ply natural cotton warp and tabby, indigo-blue and madder-rust 2-ply wool pattern wefts, 32 × 13, repeat 13 in, 85 × 77 in (checked, [SI-BRAD]). Saxon Plantation, South Carolina: 36 × 18, repeat 2.5 in, 96 × 75.5 in (checked, [SI-SAX]). Fragments 22–40 ends and 30–55 picks an inch, repeats 3.25–14.5 in (checked, [SI-FRAG]); an antique one at 44 ends (checked, [TR-ROSE]); Colonial Williamsburg, 1830–60: 40 ends, 36 cotton and 25 wool picks an inch (unchecked, snippet).
- **Summer and winter:** 23 × 35, 2-ply cotton warp, singles cotton tabby, 2-ply blue wool pattern (checked, [SI-SW]).
- **Double weave:** 22 × 22, indigo wool with unbleached cotton that "has yellowed with age", c. 1790–1820 (checked, [SI-DW]); 17 × 17 all wool (checked, [SI-DW]).
- **Jacquard** (mostly double cloth): 16–22 a layer, 34 × 36 at the finest; a single-cloth damask of 1846 at 57 × 51–55 for comparison (checked, [SI-JAC]).
- **Plain:** linsey-woolsey 24 × 34 and 27 × 26 (checked, [SI-LW]). No count found for plain indigo or madder cloth.
- **Atwater:** 20/2 cotton warp "thirty or thirty-two threads to the inch" for overshot, the pattern weft "coarser than that used for tabby" (checked, [ATW]).

So a coverlet is about 30 ends an inch (0.8 mm an end) and 13–18 pattern picks an inch, a pattern pick and its tabby 1.4–2.0 mm, about two ends tall, so figures come out square (derived).

**On the screen.** The 14-inch MacBook Pro is 3024 × 1964 at 254 ppi (checked, [APL]), 0.1 mm a pixel: an end at 30–32 an inch is 7.9–8.5 px and a pair 14–20 px. **8 px an end and 16 a pair is life size**, and the screen shows 11.9 × 7.7 in of cloth, 378 ends by 100–140 pattern picks (derived). A 27-inch 5K display (218 ppi, unchecked) makes life size about 7 px. Life size is the honest standard for Thread size, and it sits where §4 wants it.

**Threads.** No museum figure for a yarn's diameter was found. From the yarn counts (Ashenhurst's d = 1/(K√(yd per lb)) and Peirce's d = 1/(28√Ne) inches, checked [DIA]; yards per pound of each count, checked [UNITS]): 20/2 cotton about 0.29 mm, 24/3 0.33 mm, a 2-ply woollen of 1,000 yd/lb about 1 mm (derived; the woollen's count is illustrative). So the cotton warp and tabby fill little more than a third of their 0.8 mm spacing while the wool pattern weft is thick and covers: the cotton ground reads as a fine open lattice and the wool floats as soft, fat, packed bands. **The shader wants a width for each set of threads** (warp, tabby, pattern), not one for all; the probe's one width is a large part of why its stills read as basketry.

**Panels and repeats.** Coverlets "eighty-four or eighty-eight inches wide, in two strips, and the strips are woven three yards long", borders 10–14 in; a 42 in strip is 1,260 ends at 30 an inch, on a warp of at least 8 yd (Atwater, checked [ATW]). Mountain looms were "typically forty-four inches wide" (checked, [WCU]). Whole coverlets measure 80–96 by 71–83 in (checked, SI records above; Winterthur's 1836 Jacquard 86.75 × 94.5, [WIN]). Repeats run 2.5–14.5 in, most 3.25–8.6 (checked, above). So at life size the screen holds from under one repeat (Braddock's Defeat, 13 in) to nearly five (Saxon, 2.5), most often one and a half to three and a half; and a 3-yd strip is about fourteen screens tall (derived).

**How fast.**
- Guest, 1823: "A very good Hand Weaver ... will weave two pieces of nine-eighths shirting per week, each twenty-four yards long, and containing one hundred and five shoots of weft in an inch" (checked, [GUE]); a hand-loom weaver's week "seldom exceeds fifty-six or fifty-eight hours" (Baines 1835, checked [BAI]). That is about 50 picks a minute averaged over every stop, about 0.85 yd of plain cloth an hour (derived).
- Atwater: "from one to eight yards of fabric in a day's work" by hand, and a fly-shuttle loom "thirty and more yards" (checked, [ATW]); the flying shuttle roughly doubled a weaver's output (checked, [FLY]).
- Weavers now: 20–24 picks a minute at "shuttle throwing pace" and 44–48 with a fly shuttle (checked, [FRY]); 28 picks in about 45 s, about 37 a minute, plain weave on a treadle loom (checked, [CHIU]). Power looms: Cartwright's 120–130 a minute (checked, [PLOOM]).
- Coverlets: a home weaver took "six full days to complete a coverlet", warping and all (checked, [WCU]); a professional Jacquard weaver averaged three a week, one a day if rushed (checked, [HAL]).
- Overshot needs a tabby for every pattern pick, so twice the picks an inch for the same figure and about half the speed in inches; no timed figure was found (unchecked).
- So a weaver throwing two shuttles at 20–25 picks a minute lays a pattern pick and its tabby about every 5 s: at life size the cloth grows about 3 px a second, a screen in about 10 minutes, a 3-yd strip in about 2½ hours of weaving (derived).

**The loom and what the weaver sees.** The warp unrolls from the warp beam at the back, runs through the heddles and the reed in the beater, and the woven cloth passes over the breast beam to the cloth beam below it, held by a pawl and ratchet (checked, [LOOM]); "the point where the fabric is formed is called the fell" (checked, [PLOOM]). A hand weaver weaves "about two inches before advancing the warp", starting about 2 in from the breast beam, about 3 in on large Scandinavian looms, and advancing often keeps the beat at the same angle (checked, [HW]). So from the bench there are some 2–6 in of finished cloth between fell and breast beam, the rest wound away out of sight (derived); the fell climbs 2–3 in, 500–750 px at life size, and is wound back.

**Dyes.**
- Coverlet wool was "most often dyed a dark blue from indigo, but madder red, walnut brown, and a lighter 'Williamsburg blue' were also used", on undyed cotton (checked, [WCOV]). Atwater: blue the usual dye; madder gave "the lovely brownish rose shades ... in the ancient coverlets"; Jacquards were dyed with "the madder and indigo of the home dye-pot" (checked, [ATW]). Indigo and madder on natural cotton in one coverlet: Braddock's Defeat (checked, [SI-BRAD]); "indigo blue or orange-y madder red are much more common" (checked, [TR-ROSE]); where the two cross, "a darker blue-red" (checked, [TR-JAC]).
- Indigo's depth is by the number of dips ("keep on dipping and airing ... until it gits as dark as you want", checked [HAL]; Bronson the same, checked [ATW]). No measured colour a dip was found.
- Madder on wool after an alum boil (checked, [HAL]); with iron, or over indigo, purples (checked, [RUB]); on cotton with alum, reds and pinks (checked, [VAM]).
- **Measured,** modern reference dyeings on wool under D65 (checked, [BIO]): madder with alum L\* 51, a\* 23, b\* 40, sRGB 173, 104, 52; madder unmordanted 168, 114, 75; woad (indigo's own blue) 88, 117, 138, then 66, 93, 102, and the darkest 36, 52, 63. Nothing measured from historic coverlets, from iron-mordanted madder or from undyed cotton was found. Madder on wool measures orange-brown, not crimson: the family is rust to brownish rose, as Atwater says. Old natural cotton has yellowed (checked, [SI-DW]).

## 3. The view and the motion

**Four views, judged against Dave's direction:**
- **(A) The cloth flat on, still, the fell rising up the screen** as picks go in, bare warp above it, and when the fell reaches the top the cloth rolls on. Rejected: for most of each round the screen is mostly bare warp, the empty sheet that cut Peg Board, and it is Etching's act exactly (a picture built from nothing, held, cleared). The probe's first still shows what the bare part looks like: a flat field of fine vertical stripes, the dullest thing on the screen.
- **(B) The cloth flat on, the fell fixed near the top, the cloth going down as it is woven.** Recommended. Woven cloth fills four fifths of the screen or more at every moment; the newest pick goes in at the fell; above it a band of open warp (a finger's width to a few centimetres) runs up to the top edge, where the reed would be. The cloth below moves down at the rate it is woven, as it does onto the breast beam, and leaves by the bottom edge. "Rolling on to the next" is then the cloth itself: when one piece ends, the weaver weaves a few picks of plain heading (or leaves a gap of warp for a fringe) and the next piece begins at the fell, with the end of the last still filling the screen below it. No reset, no hold, no clear, never empty, and every change of cloth is a seam passing slowly down the screen. It is also the only view where nothing has to be drawn but cloth.
- **(C) The loom from the weaver's bench,** in perspective: the breast beam across the bottom, the cloth running away to the fell, the beater and reed, the shed open with the warp in two layers, the shuttle passing. The most "loom", and the weakest bet: a drawn machine is what sank A Tree for the Year ("photoreal wins"); the cloth is foreshortened, so its thread size shrinks toward the fell and is sure to alias there (§4); and the beater's swing and the shuttle's throw are the biggest, fastest motions in the scene, against "calm". A photo of a real loom with the cloth drawn into it is possible (the cut-out-and-relight scenes' method) but matching the shader's cloth to a photo's perspective and light is a project of its own. Show it once on the sheet, as the alternative.
- **(D) Close, at four to six times life size,** a few dozen threads across: the texture as the subject, the fibres and twist visible, the figure too big to see whole. Not a view of its own; it is the top of the Thread size slider in (B).

**How a pick goes in.** On a hand loom, per pick: the treadle changes the shed (half the warp rises, half falls), the shuttle is thrown through and caught (left to right, then right to left the next pick), the weft is laid at a slight angle or in a bubble so it can take up round the warp, and the beater brings it to the fell, once or twice. Seen flat on, the shed change shows only in the band of open warp, and the beat moves the new pick a few millimetres. So on screen: the new pick appears as the shuttle crosses (the weft drawn across with a soft front, from the side the last one ended on), then settles the last few pixels into the fell over a fraction of a second, eased, no bounce. In overshot the pattern and tabby shuttles alternate, so the colour of the pick that crosses alternates too: indigo, natural, indigo. The probe draws the pick in progress with a soft front three ends wide (`u_rows`, its fraction the shuttle's place); the settle is not drawn yet.

**How the cloth advances.** On a hand loom of the coverlet era the cloth does not creep: the weaver weaves until the fell is a few inches from the reed, then releases the warp beam and winds the cloth beam on by a ratchet (§2). So the honest picture is the fell rising slowly for minutes and then the whole cloth winding down by a few inches at once. Two choices for the mock-ups: **steady**, the cloth going down continuously at the weaving rate (the calmest: nothing on the screen moves faster than a pixel every half second at the standard pace), or **as a weaver works**, the fell creeping up by a few centimetres and the cloth wound on every few minutes (a slow pan of a few hundred pixels over several seconds). Either way the motion is in whole device pixels (§4).

**Pace.** At a weaver's own pace (§2) a pick crosses every 2½ s and the cloth grows about 3 px a second: real, and busy for a desktop. Proposed standard, half that: a pattern pick and its tabby every 10 s, the cloth going down 1.6 px a second (one whole pixel about every 0.6 s), a screen woven in about 20 minutes, a 6 in figure in 15–20 minutes, a 3-yd strip in about 4½ hours (derived). The Speed knob runs from a strip a day (3,240 picks, one every 27 s; derived from Atwater's strip at 15 pattern picks an inch) up to a weaver's pace, with a few times faster for a demo. Variety the way weavers got it: each strip a different treadling of the same threading (as drawn in, rose fashion, star fashion; §1), a new warp (draft and colours) after each coverlet, and a new one on each load.

**Battery.** At the standard pace the cloth moves about 1–2 px a second, so the cloth itself is well under 2 device pixels a frame even at 10 fps (`batteryRate` 10 would do for it). The shuttle's crossing is the only fast thing: a soft front crossing 3024 px in a second or two is 100–300 px a frame at 10 fps, which steps. Two ways: make the crossing a fade across with a wide soft front, so 15 fps steps don't show (one cap, `batteryRate` 15), or adopt `BatteryPaced` (as Night Sky's meteors): 10 fps between picks, raised for the second each pick takes to cross. Which looks right is a mock-up question; the second costs a few lines.

## 4. Shimmer, the named risk

**Why a weave shimmers.** A woven cloth is a pattern with a fixed period, the thread pitch, and features finer than that inside it: the dark gap between threads, the round of each thread, the dip where a float goes under. Drawn by sampling each pixel once, the part of that detail finer than two pixels can't be shown and folds back as coarser false patterns. On a still picture whose pitch is a whole number of pixels, every repeat lands on the pixel grid the same way, so the error is the same in every repeat and simply looks like a slightly crisper cloth. Three things break that: a pitch that is not a whole number of pixels (every few threads the phase drifts, giving bands: static moiré); moving the cloth by a fraction of a pixel (the error changes from frame to frame: crawl, and a pumping of the whole cloth's brightness); and drawing it at another scale (the Settings preview, reduced stills and the menu tile).

Moiré in graphics is "a special case of aliasing, due to undersampling a fine regular pattern", overcome by mipmapping and anisotropic filtering (checked, [MOIRE]).

**What the probe measured.** Each frame was compared with the same frame drawn at 4 × 4 samples a pixel and averaged (the truth), both seen through a blur of about 2 px (what the eye makes of fine detail at a desk), in the display's 8-bit levels. Over eight offsets of the cloth: **err**, the structured difference (the RMS less its mean); **move**, how much that difference changes from one offset to the next once the cloth's own movement is taken out (crawl); **pump**, how far the mean brightness of the whole frame swings (the cloth breathing as it slides). A whole-screen swing of 1 level near mid-grey is about 1.6% of the light (derived: luminance goes as the level squared in the shader's encoding), and a large field flickering by about 1% at 1–2 Hz is near the threshold of seeing it (unchecked, from memory of flicker sensitivity curves). So pump of 1 or more is a visible breathing; move of 1 or more is a visible crawl.

**In the Settings preview,** drawn at 0.375 of the desktop's size (`LivePreview` builds the scene at the display's size and scales it to fill a header 340 pt tall, so 0.35 or more by the window's width, app), twill, whole preview-pixel steps, filtered: where the pitch lands on whole preview pixels, nothing moves (8 px → 3 preview px: err 0.17, move 0.00; 5⅓ → 2: 0.23, 0.00). Where it does not, it bands: 4 px → 1.5 preview px err 0.59, move 0.49; 6 px → 2.25 err 2.74; 12 px → 4.5 err 1.57. Unfiltered with fractional steps it pumps by 4.5–29 levels at 4–12 px. Overshot at 8 px, whose pattern and tabby rows are 12 and 4 desktop px but 4.5 and 1.5 preview px, crawls even at whole steps (filtered err 4.5, move 3.8, pump 2.0): **every boundary has to land on a whole pixel, the rows' as well as the ends'** (all measured). `docs/loom/probe-preview.jpg` shows it (drawn in the first run: hard look, pattern share 0.62): the 4 px twill and overshot at preview scale, unfiltered on the left (vertical bands across the twill; the overshot's ground striped), filtered on the right (the twill's diagonal clean, the overshot's ground even).

**Reduced stills.** The first still at a quarter size, averaged by area, is clean; the same reduced by nearest pixel shows beats in the open warp above the fell; at the menu tile's 178 × 116 px, by Lanczos as `site/media.sh` does, a twill becomes a flat grey field and the overshot a mottled figure, with no moiré seen by eye in either (looked at in the probe's sheets, not kept). So stills must be reduced by area or Lanczos, never by nearest or plain bilinear, or drawn at the size they are shown at, as Peg Board's were (`pegBoardStill`, app history). A twill alone makes a dull tile: the tile should show a figured cloth.

**The rules that follow.**
1. **Thread size in whole device pixels, and every row too.** The end pitch, the pattern pick's height and the tabby's are each a whole number of device pixels (at 8 px: 8, 12 and 4). Thread size is a slider of whole pixels, not a continuous one. Measured: zero crawl and zero pumping at every pitch tried, filtered or not, as long as the next rule holds.
2. **The cloth never moves by a fraction of a pixel.** It advances in whole device pixels (one step every half second or so at the standard pace), and the settle of a new pick is drawn in the pick, not by moving the cloth. A fractional move pumps the whole cloth by 0.75–14 levels, more at small sizes and unfiltered (measured above).
3. **At least 6 px an end at 2x**, 8 by default. At 3–4 px the static error is fine but the preview can't hold the pattern (1.1–1.5 preview px an end) and a fractional move pumps by 4–14 levels; at 6 px and over every case is quiet at whole steps. Life size on a 14-inch screen is about 8 px (§2).
4. **The shader filters by its own footprint anyway** (`fwidth` of the pixel's position in cloth space, times π/4 as the app's `fwidth` runs high): coverage across a thread box-filtered exactly; the round, the dive and the twist faded to their means as a thread nears 2 px; and the crossings themselves faded to a baked mean colour (4 × 4 crossings a texel, smoothed) as a crossing nears a pixel. It halves the pumping where something does move by a fraction, and keeps the preview, stills and scaled display modes sane. It costs 0.016 ms at most (0.299 against 0.283 ms at 4 px, nothing at 8). SpriteKit's own mipmaps (`usesMipmaps`) were not tried; a baked mean texture doesn't depend on whether a custom shader's reads use them.
5. **The preview snaps too.** The scene knows its own scale (the view's size against the scene's), and the shader rounds the end pitch and both row heights to whole preview pixels, two at the least. Measured: snapped twill at whole steps err 0.06–0.52, move 0.00, at every pitch tried. The overshot's rows need snapping each, which the probe did not do.
6. **Soft cloth is the second cure.** Packed threads with shallow gaps and gentle relief have less contrast at the thread's own frequency, so less to fold back. Raking light, which brings the weave out, does the opposite: keep the light broad.

**What can't be controlled: scaled display modes.** On "More Space" (a 14-inch screen set to look like 1800 × 1169) macOS draws at 3600 × 2338 and the WindowServer shrinks every frame by 0.84 to the panel (unchecked as a figure for this Mac; the mechanism is how macOS scaled modes work). An end of 8 px becomes 6.7 physical px, and whatever filter the WindowServer uses resamples it every frame: static beats every three to four ends, and a whole-pixel step in the drawing is a fractional one on the glass. Rule 6 and a size of 8 px or more are the only defence. Test on such a mode before calling it done.

## 5. How it would be drawn

**Engine: one full-screen `SKShader` reading a drawdown texture, the pattern of Peg Board's per-cell shader**, and none of the others: there is nothing to simulate (Schlieren, Meander's river), no photo to relight (the cloth is regular by nature, and a photo of cloth could neither grow nor change pattern), and the pixel canvas is for pixel art. It borrows from three that exist: Peg Board's shader (git `51a8a7f`: per-cell texels read at their centre, cost by the pixel, not by the number of cells), Etching's clock (`Etching.Cycle`: the place in the work kept in defaults and worked out from the wall clock, so a relaunch, Shuffle or a second display carry on from the same place), and `WallpaperTime`'s `u_now` for anything that wanders. A custom scene with one sprite and its uniforms set only when they change (Peg Board's finding: setting three uniforms every frame was 2.7% of a frame, app history).

**Baked, once per cloth, off the main thread** (milliseconds, so the main thread would do, but a cloth can change while the menu is open): the draft read from its file; one repeat of the drawdown as an RGBA texel per crossing (flags, the top thread's colour index, a random number for the thread; the probe's layout); the warp's colours, a texel per end; a palette texture of eight colours; the mean-colour texture, 4 × 4 crossings a texel. The shader takes the repeat with `mod`, so a texture of one repeat covers any screen. A change of cloth is a second set of textures and the row it begins on; the shader picks by row. Draft files are tiny (a WIF is a line a thread, a few kilobytes; derived), so any number can ship.

**Live, per pixel** (the probe's `cloth` shader, about 60 lines): the pixel's place in cloth space (ends across; pattern-and-tabby pairs up, plus the cloth's advance); one read of the crossing at its texel's centre (SpriteKit smooths textures whatever the filtering mode, app); the across and along coordinates of whichever thread is on top; a round profile across it lit from above left, a dip where the float dives under, a faint twist along it, a gain per thread for the unevenness of handspun; the gap between threads dark; the band above the fell drawn as open warp (a read of the warp's colours); the pick in progress drawn up to the shuttle's place; and the footprint filter (§4). Fuzz (wool's halo of fibres) and the threads' wander were not in the probe; both belong in cloth space and fade with the footprint like the twist. Per frame the CPU sets the rows woven and the advance, and nothing else.

**No `HeadStart` needed:** the cloth is already woven when the scene opens, so Settings' copy and the desktop's are judged on the same thing.

**The clock.** Picks woven = (now − the cloth's start) × pace, so the place in a cloth is the wall's, shared by every display through defaults (Etching's pattern). Advance = picks woven × their heights, rounded down to a whole pixel. The rounding is what keeps rule 2: it moves the cloth by one pixel at a time whatever the frame rate, so it is the same at 15, 30, 60 and 120 fps by construction.

## 6. Cost (measured)

The probe (`main.swift` in the session's scratch folder, not kept in the repo) drives `SKRenderer` as Peg Board's bench did: 3024 × 1964, the GPU time from the command buffer, the CPU the frame's wall time less that (so SpriteKit's own work is in it), medians of 40 frames after 40 at 60 fps steps, the fell rising and the cloth moving as live. **Calibration:** Peg Board's own shader from its bench, with stand-in textures, reads 0.340 ms here against 0.38 in its doc, so this run reads about 10% low.

| Scene | GPU, ms | CPU, ms |
|---|---|---|
| Empty scene (SpriteKit's floor) | 0.011 | 0.345 |
| Peg Board's shader (its doc: 0.38) | 0.340 | 0.337 |
| Flat: the top thread's colour only, 4 / 8 px | 0.132 / 0.105 | 0.367 / 0.347 |
| The cloth shader, overshot, 4 / 8 px, filtered | 0.299 / 0.283 | 0.365 / 0.340 |
| The same unfiltered | 0.283 / 0.283 | 0.341 / 0.336 |
| The cloth shader, twill, 4 / 8 px | 0.283 / 0.283 | 0.339 / 0.337 |
| The cloth shader, overshot, 8 px, at 6016 × 3384 (6K) | 0.943 | 0.365 |

So the whole wallpaper is about 0.3 ms of GPU and SpriteKit's floor of CPU at 2x on a 14-inch screen, a seventh of the budget, and under 1 ms on a 6K display. Thread size changes nothing (the cost is by the pixel), and fuzz, wander and the pick's settle would add a fraction. No `batteryScale`: the threads are the detail, and there is little to save.

## 7. Risks, worst first
1. **Shimmer.** Solved on paper and measured at 1:1 by rules 1–3 (whole-pixel threads, rows and steps); left to test on scaled display modes, which no rule reaches, and in the preview, where the overshot's rows still crawled in the probe until snapped.
2. **It reads as a graphic, not cloth.** The probe's hard look reads as basketry or rattan: stiff round rods with black gaps. Real coverlet wool is fuzzy, packed, and its floats lie flat and slightly irregular. This is the fault that cut Sea Sparkle and Crystals: the main thing in the picture didn't pass. Mock-ups must be judged beside photos of real coverlets at the same scale.
3. **Too close to Etching and Peg Board.** Any version that builds a picture from a bare screen and clears it repeats both. View (B) has no bare screen and no clear; the cloth's subject is its texture and figure, endless, not a picture that finishes. Keep it so: no "finished coverlet" hold.
4. **Too still to notice, or too busy.** A pick every few seconds may read as nothing happening; a crossing every second as fidget. The pace and the crossing's look are for Dave to judge in films, not stills.
5. **Bright ground.** Natural cotton is the brightest thing in a coverlet; a cloth of half white on the desktop is not dim. The look needs a dim, warm natural, and an indigo ground (the negative) as a choice.
6. **Drafts' rights.** The easy source, handweaving.net, has no licence and is behind a subscription; every draft has to be transcribed from a public-domain scan (Bronson, Worst, the German books; Atwater in the US only until 2027), which is hand work, a few hours a draft with checking. Budget for a dozen to start.
7. **Cost on big displays:** under 1 ms at 6K; not a risk.

## 8. For the mock-ups
Questions the sheets should put to Dave:
1. **The view:** (B) cloth flat on with the fell near the top and the cloth going down, against (C) the loom from the bench. And how much open warp above the fell: a hair's width, two centimetres, or a band with the reed's line.
2. **Kinds of draft:** overshot alone, or with summer and winter, twills and double weave; each shown as a still of a real draft.
3. **Colours:** indigo on natural, madder on natural, and indigo with madder (the classic two-colour coverlet, as Braddock's Defeat is, [SI-BRAD]; Dave's rule is one family unless argued for, and the argument is that it is what the old coverlets did), each as one cloth; and a dark ground (indigo warp) against a light one.
4. **Thread size:** life size (about 8 px on a 14-inch screen) against twice that, shown at 1:1 crops, not reduced.
5. **The look of the thread:** soft (packed, fuzzy, shallow) against crisp (rounder, deeper gaps), beside a photo of a real coverlet.
6. **Pace:** films at a weaver's real pace, at a calm pace and at a cloth a day; and the Speed knob's range.
7. **The pick:** a shuttle front crossing against a fade across; with or without the settle into the fell.
8. **The cloth going on:** steady, or as a weaver works (creep, then wound on); and how one cloth gives way to the next: a heading of plain weave, a fringe of bare warp, or a new treadling on the same warp (real weavers vary the treadling on one threading to get several patterns from one warp).
