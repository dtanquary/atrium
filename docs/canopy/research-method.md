# Canopy: the method research

A research agent's report, 2026-11-16, on how to draw a forest canopy seen from the floor looking straight up within the app's budget: the camera and the sky, the real thing in numbers, three ways to draw the crowns and how each takes the seasons, what it costs (measured), what is baked and what runs live, the risks, and what to mock up. Nothing here is built. The look (palettes, brightness caps, photos to cut out, names) is the other report, `research-look.md`.

**How to read the marks.** **Checked** means read on the page whose URL follows (or in the source list at the end); "abstract" or "snippet" where only that was seen. **Unchecked** means no page was read that states it. **Derived** is my arithmetic on the figures named; **measured** is the probe below, on this Mac (M3 Max); **app** means read in this repo's code.

## Recommendation in ten lines
1. **The view:** straight up through a 90° pinhole lens, lying in a small gap of the stand: the nearest trunks run in from the edges as wedges converging on the middle, and a ring of 15–25 crowns closes round channels of sky (§1, §2).
2. **Drawing, the hybrid (c):** a real bare stand from a straight-up photo as the wood (trunks, limbs, twigs, its own crown shyness and evergreens), with scanned leaves (ambientCG, CC0) grown on its twigs at load in two or three layers, and one shader for the sky, the leaves, their season and their light. Stars and falling leaves are sprites (§3).
3. **Seasons from one bake:** each spray has an id, read against its species' calendar, so leaf-out, colour (from real autumn scans), fall (sooner in gusts), bare lace and snow on the branches are one continuous change. No photo per season; the Season setting holds one date (§3).
4. **Light by physics:** each leaf is lit by what comes through it from above. The app's own `Atmosphere` puts a sunlit leaf at about the clear zenith's brightness, so leaves glow and go dark only where the crown is thick. That is the first softener, before any exposure (§1, §2).
5. **Motion:** each crown sways as a body at 0.15–0.3 Hz in leaf (19% faster and half as damped when bare), 4–11 px on screen, its amplitude set by the live wind and gusts. Aspen's 3–5 Hz flutter shows as a shimmer of light. Falling leaves come about one at a time at the peak, in bursts in a gust (§2).
6. **Cost, measured:** the whole hybrid is 0.41 ms of GPU and 0.48 ms of CPU at 3024 × 1964 (Nebula 1.72 in the same run), and a heavier five-layer shader is 0.66 ms of GPU. The bake is 0.2 s on the GPU or 0.5 s in Core Graphics, with about 90 MB of texture (§3).
7. **Battery:** `BatteryPaced` at 12–15 fps in calm weather and 20 in wind, lifted while a leaf falls, with flutter fading out below 15. No `batteryScale`: the leaves are the detail, and a 0.75 trim saves 0.15 ms (§3).
8. **Not (a) alone:** photo crowns can't thin to their own limbs or go bare, so they'd need a different stand each season. **Not (b) alone:** limbs laid out by code are what sank A Tree for the Year. Mock all three beside a real photo before anything else (§5).
9. **Data:** `Phenology` (white oak) plus per-species offsets from Harvard Forest, the Yale catalogue, and Open-Meteo, now with current gusts and 72 hours of snow history. New code: a zenith `SkyCamera` (§1, §2).
10. **Worst risks:** the trees read drawn; the contrast swings with the season (summer leaves against winter lace on white); falling leaves come at the viewer, the motion Dave turned down for rain (§4).

## 1. The camera and the sky
**The lens.** A rectilinear (pinhole) camera pointed at the zenith, 90° across the long side of the screen, as a phone's ultra-wide or a 16–18 mm lens: then a 1512 × 982 pt screen is 66° tall and 100° across the diagonal, and shows 1.58 sr, 12.6% of the whole sky. At 2x (3024 px across) that is 1512 px per radian, 26.4 px a degree at the centre (all derived). A pinhole keeps straight things straight, which matters here: every vertical line in the forest runs to one vanishing point, the screen's centre.
- **Trunks are wedges.** A trunk of width *w* standing *d* m off reaches the screen at radius 1512·*d*/*h* px and is 1512·*w*/*h* px wide at height *h*, so on screen it is a wedge of fixed angle *w*/*d* pointing at the centre: 0.3 m at 6 m is 2.9° (76 px wide where it enters the screen, at *h* = *d* on the long axis), narrowing in proportion as it climbs, less a little for the trunk's own taper (derived). Its bark is laid along it by height, *h* = 1512·*d*/*r*, so it foreshortens toward the crown. One sprite a trunk with the wedge and the bark mapping in its shader, or a polar pass in the canopy shader; nothing a trunk does moves it but a lean with its crown.
- **Leaves are a few pixels.** A leaf of *L* at height *h* is 1512·*L*/*h* px: a 10 cm leaf at 20 m is 7.6 px (3.8 pt), 12 cm at 15 m is 12 px, a shade leaf of 15 cm at 12 m 19 px (derived). So a leaf's own outline barely reads; what reads is the spray (0.3–0.6 m: 25–75 px), the clump and the limb. Photo or scan detail matters at the scale of sprays and limbs, and at the scale of leaves only for the falling ones near the camera.
- **Where the crowns are.** A crown stands over its trunk, so a tree *d* m away shows its crown at radius 1512·*d*/*h*: the nearest trees' crowns fill the middle, and trees beyond *d* ≈ *h* lie off the long sides. The shot the brief describes (trunks running in from the edges, crowns ringing a channel of sky) is lying in a small gap of the stand, with no tree within about 4–8 m; under closed canopy the middle is the nearest crown's underside instead (derived; which to show is a mock-up question).

**The sky at the zenith (`Atmosphere`, app).** `SkyLight.bake` marches 128 × 96 rays through Hillaire's atmosphere in about 20 ms (app), but its `SkyCamera` only looks level; a zenith camera is a new `ray(u, v)` (right × *x*·tan 45° + north × *y*·tan 33° + up), and everything else (exposure, sun and sky colours, star fading by `skyLuminance`) carries over. Run through the app's own `Atmosphere` (probe, `sky/main.swift`): the clear zenith's luminance is 0.06–0.10 of a level surface's direct sunlight over π for the Sun 20–70° up (0.19 at 10°), and its colour is (0.13, 0.27, 0.60) of its sum, deep blue, with the sky 30° off the zenith 1.5–2.2 times as bright toward the Sun and 0.86–1.2 away from it (measured, the app's model). **What that means for the leaves:** a leaf transmits a few percent of the light on its top side, diffusely (§2), so a sunlit leaf seen from below has a luminance of about T × E/π, the same order as the zenith; a leaf in the shade of others gets the sky's diffuse light or light already through one leaf, a tenth or less. The physics gives "leaves glow rather than go black" for the sunlit edges and gaps' rims, and dark cores only where the crown is thick (derived from the measured ratio and §2's T).

**The Sun in frame.** Through the 33° half-height of the short side, the Sun is in frame whenever it stands over 57° up; through the 45° of the long side, over 45°. At 42°N that is noon from 13 April to 28 August across the short side, or 14 March to 27 September across the long side (derived from the noon altitude 48° + declination). So put the north–south line on the screen's short side (head toward the pole), and the Sun shows only around summer middays. When it does, it is behind leaves most of the time: its light through a gap is the flash risk (§4), so its visibility should come from a blurred coverage map, fading in and out over seconds, not from the pixel under it.

**Stars through the gaps (app's catalogue).** `stars.txt` holds the Yale Bright Star Catalogue's 2,888 stars to V 5.5, public domain (its header; the app already credits it). A 12.6% share of the sky holds about 360 of them, more in summer when the Milky Way crosses the zenith at mid-northern latitudes (derived); through a summer canopy's quarter to a third of sky that is about 90–130, through winter's lace perhaps 250. Each a sprite (`NightSky.addStars`' sizes and colours), turned to the zenith frame with `Sky.horizonMatrix`, between the sky and the canopy so the leaves hide them: measured, 350 star sprites cost nothing visible on the GPU (0.08 ms with the sky) and are batched. The sky turns 0.08 px a second at the zenith at 42°N (15.04°/h × cos 42°, derived), so placing them once a second is invisible.

**Clouds overhead.** Weather's photo cumulus are side views and don't fit a view straight up, where a cumulus is its flat grey base. Weather's procedural deck (`cloudShape` over `CloudNoise`, a ray meeting a plane at the cloud base) works unchanged looking up, and it is what a canopy shows of the weather anyway: grey deck, broken cumulus bases, cirrus streaks. Measured with the sky: 0.08 ms.

## 2. The real thing in numbers
Gathered by a second agent for this report and marked as it read them (source keys at the end). Where no page was found, it says so.

**The trees.**
- Heights: sugar maple 27–37 m, red maple 18–27 m, American beech 18–24 m (to 37), quaking aspen 20–25 m in mature stands (checked, [SIL]).
- Crown width of trees grown in stands, from Bechtold's equations at a typical diameter (derived from checked coefficients, [BEC]): sugar maple 8.2 m at 41 cm, red maple 7.6 m at 36 cm, red oak 9.6 m at 46 cm, white oak about 10 m (its coefficients came out garbled in the text, so unchecked), beech 8.4 m at 36 cm, paper birch 5.7 m at 25 cm, aspen 5.3 m, white pine 8.0 m at 51 cm, hemlock 7.5 m at 41 cm. Birch and aspen crowns are about two thirds the width of the broadleaves', so a birch grove is finer-grained overhead.
- Spacing: 390–520 stems a hectare at 30 cm mean diameter (4.4–5.1 m apart), 220–290 at 40 cm (5.8–6.7 m), from sugar maple's stocking (derived, [SIL]). So the nearest trunks are 3–7 m off: their trunks run in from the edges as 2–5° wedges (§1), and at 20 m up a screen 40 × 26 m across holds about 15–25 broadleaf crowns, or twice as many birches (derived).
- Crown depth: a live-crown ratio of about 40% in young northern hardwood stands (checked, [NYL]); none found for mature closed stands (unchecked). At 40%, a 25 m tree's crown starts at 15 m.

**Crown shyness.** Little in lodgepole pine stands under 10 m tall, significant over 15 m, from crowns striking each other in wind (checked, [FISH]); stands 20 m tall were under 50% closed, so "less than 50% of the view of the sky may be covered by crowns" (checked, [RUD-T]); swaying and whipping make it (Goudie, checked abstract, [GOU]). **No measured gap width in metres was found, and none for any broadleaf** (unchecked): photos suggest channels of about half a metre to a metre, 40–75 px at 20 m (estimate). The brief's quarter to a third of sky by day is in line with a gap in the stand plus shyness channels.

**Sway.**
- Whole trees: frequency scales as diameter/height² (Jackson et al. 2019), but loosely for temperate broadleaves; a modelled sycamore 0.26 Hz, measured temperate broadleaves in leaf mostly 0.15–0.3 Hz (read off their figure by eye); 19% higher out of leaf; damped 8.6% of critical in leaf and 3.9% bare, so a bare crown rings on for twice as many swings after a gust (checked, [JAC]). The frequency doesn't change with the wind (snippet, [BUN]); the amplitude does.
- Amplitude: Scots pines 9.5 and 13.5 m tall moved under 2 and 5 cm at their crowns' centres in 4–8 m/s winds (Peltola 1993, as cited, checked, [CAR]). For 20–25 m hardwoods in a breeze, 5–15 cm at the crown (estimate), which at 20 m up is 4–11 px on screen (derived). Small: the motion is a slow drift of whole crowns against each other, with gusts.
- Branches: no frequency found (unchecked; Dappled Light's research gave about 1 Hz).
- Aspen flutter: 3–5 Hz in 2–4 m/s of wind, starting at about 3 m/s or less (checked, second-hand [POP]; snippet [ROD]). The aspen grove's signature, and Dappled Light found flutter that fast reads as flicker, so it should be a soft shimmer of light, not of place.
- Wind in the canopy against Open-Meteo's 10 m wind: not found (unchecked); A Tree for the Year used 0.9 of it (app, git `ed079db^`).

**Falling leaves.**
- How they fall: tumbling, fluttering or chaotic, like falling discs, with the speed of a tumbling one varying by about 60% within each turn (checked, [FIE]); most of 25 leaf shapes settle at about a disc's rate (checked, [BIV]). No speed in m/s was found for a single leaf: 0.8–1.0 m/s for a plate of 50–80 g/m² (derived from assumed inputs), and leafy twigs of *Zelkova* fall at 1.5 m/s (snippet, [CER]), an upper bound.
- Where: 90% of litter lands within 17 m of its tree (snippet, [FER]).
- When: autumn brings half a year's litterfall at Hubbard Brook, and "storms were very important in the timing" (checked abstract, [GOS]), which is Dave's "more in gusts". Weekly shares at the peak: not found (unchecked).
- How many at once: a canopy of leaf area 5 (Harvard Forest, [RIC]) of 75–150 cm² leaves (unchecked) drops 330–670 leaves a square metre, most of them in about four weeks (estimate); falling at 1 m/s through the view between 5 and 15 m up (2,800 m³ of it, derived from §1's lens), that is **about one leaf in view at a time at the peak, a new one every 15 s or so**, and bursts of tens in a gust (derived). Few: a `BatteryPaced` rate lifted while one falls, as Night Sky's meteors, costs little.

**How much sky.**
- Light reaching the floor: under 2% of full sun under beech and hemlock, over 5% under red oak (checked, [CAN]); about 15% under silver birch and sessile oak, 1–3% under beech, lime and hornbeam (checked, [HAG]). So the birch grove is the open, bright one and the beech wood the darkest.
- Leaf area: about 5 at Harvard Forest ([RIC]); light extinction coefficient 0.57 for birch, 0.43–0.48 for beech, 0.47 for broadleaves on average; leafless oak stands have a wood area of 0.43–2.45, 7–40% of their summer total (checked, [BRE]). By Beer's law that is about 10% of sky in summer and 32–82% bare (derived, rough: the coefficient is for total sunlight, not gaps overhead). A small gap in the stand lifts the summer figure to the brief's quarter to a third.

**Light through leaves.** A leaf absorbs about 70–77% of light from 350 to 720 nm, sun and shade leaves alike (checked abstract, [HAG]), so reflection and transmission share 23–30%; single-leaf transmittance in the green of about 10–15%, peaking near 550 nm, is from memory (unchecked; Knapp & Carter 1998 has it, not read). The underside reflects more than the top, and green and red absorption falls clearly in October (checked, [SF]); in a maple's autumn, chlorophyll falls slowly for 20–30 days, anthocyanins build over about 20, then chlorophyll falls fast (snippet). For the picture: with T anywhere from 5% to 15%, a sunlit leaf seen from below is 0.5 to 2 times the clear zenith's brightness (derived with §1's measured ratio), so the method holds whatever T turns out to be; the colour of the light through a leaf should be read off a real transmittance spectrum or the ambientCG scans' translucency maps, not chosen.

**The seasons, by species** (Harvard Forest, 42.5°N, 12 years, checked [RIC]):
- Leaf-out: aspen, paper birch and sugar maple first; red maple and the other birches next; beech, red oak and white oak last. Leaves reach 75% of full size from day 134 (aspen) to 182 (white pine); beech expands fastest, in 14 days.
- Colour (half the leaves turned): red maple first, day 274 (about 1 October), aspen last, day 292 (about 19 October), paper birch between, sugar maple, beech and red oak late. Half the canopy's leaves are down by day 289 on average, and the order of colour predicts the order of fall (ρ = 0.95). So aspen doesn't turn before the oaks there.
- `Phenology` (app) is white oak's alone, from USA-NPN records for northern Illinois at 42°N: onset 278, peak 301, half down 313. The other species need their offsets from it: Harvard Forest's own records (HF003, CC0, snippet) give each species against that forest's oaks, to apply to `whiteOak`. Unchecked until read.
- **Latitude:** `Phenology` moves spring 4 days a degree and autumn 3 (Hopkins, app); PhenoCam's measured rates for deciduous forest are gentler, 2.7 days later a degree north in spring and 2.0 earlier in autumn (checked, [PHC]). At 50°N against 42°N that is 8 to 10 days different. Worth reconciling when Canopy uses it (Pixel Railway uses it too).
- Beech and young oak hold dead leaves through winter (marcescence; snippet only, no figures).
- Europe: PEP725's data, once non-commercial only, now carry a licence a dataset (CC BY, CC BY-NC, CC BY-SA, CC0 or Etalab; checked, [PEP]), so check each before use.

**Snow on branches.**
- Conifers: maximum load = α(0.27 + 46/ρ)·LAI with α = 5.9 kg/m² for pine and spruce, unloading exponentially toward zero "over a few days" (Hedstrom & Pomeroy 1998); frequent unloading as soon as the air goes above 0 °C (Storck 2000); wind removes 33–100% of a dry, cold load; rain washes it off; snow sticks best between −3 and 0 °C (checked, [LUN]). A conifer of leaf area 3 holds about 13–17 mm of water as snow (derived).
- Leafless hardwoods: not found (unchecked). They hold far less, mostly sticky snow at −3 to 0 °C in still air.
- **So:** snow shows on the broadleaves after a fall near freezing in still air, and is gone within hours above 0 °C or in wind; on the evergreens it builds into heavy masses and stays for days. From the live weather: Open-Meteo returns 72 hours of hourly snowfall, temperature, wind and sunshine in one call with `past_days=2`, and the current gusts (`wind_gusts_10m`), checked by a call on 2026-11-16 from the app's test location; so the load can be worked out at launch with nothing saved.

## 3. How to draw it

### Measured
A throwaway probe, `$TMPDIR/canopy-probe/main.swift` (kept, not app code), drives `SKRenderer` as the Kelp Forest probe did: 3024 × 1964, GPU from the command buffer's `gpuStartTime`/`gpuEndTime`, CPU the frame's wall time less that (so it includes SpriteKit's own update and encoding; the empty scene's 0.38 ms is that floor), medians of 60 frames after 40, stepped at 60 fps, small variants run three times. **Calibration:** Nebula's shader, read from Shaders.swift, reads 1.72 ms here (the Kelp probe's run 1.49, the README's 1.6), so this run reads about 15% high. The canopy layers are baked from Dappled Light's real leaf atlas (`dappled-leaves.png`) as sprays of 9–13 leaves, four layers each the screen plus 10% to sway into, packed two to an RGBA texture (coverage and an id per spray; id 0 is wood), with each layer's coverage at an eighth of the resolution for the light from above. The look is a placeholder; the cost is what was measured.

| B, the 4 layers at half resolution | 0.34 | 0.39 |

**The bake**, four layers in parallel at full resolution plus margin (3326 × 2160 each): 6,000 sprays a layer cost 760–820 ms by Core Graphics' clip-and-fill (an id colour per spray), 505–575 ms by plain image draws of 192 pre-tinted stamps, and **180–205 ms drawn as sprites by `SKRenderer` into an offscreen texture and read back**; at half resolution, 1,500 sprays a layer, 100–140 ms (measured). Kelp Forest's five slabs took about 0.3 s.

What the numbers show:
- **Drawing is not the problem.** The whole recommended scene is about a fifth of the GPU budget and a quarter of the CPU's, and the heavy shader a third of the GPU's. There is room for the look to spend: a second light read, a depth-of-field blur on near leaves, more layers.
- **Baked layers cost the same however dense** (texture reads, not leaves), so the bake can be as rich as its load time allows.
- **Photo crowns cost by their overdraw**, as Kelp found for fronds and Jellyfish for bells: 14 overlapping crowns 0.5 ms, 24 0.8 ms, and 64 MB of texture for four distinct 2048² crowns.
- **Sprites are cheap at these counts.** Falling leaves posed every frame cost under 0.05 ms of CPU for 250; the scene needs one or two at once, tens in a gust (§2).
- **The bake belongs on the GPU or off the main thread:** 0.2 s on the main thread (Fish Tank's build is 0.34–0.58 s), or 0.5 s in Core Graphics on a background queue with the sky showing first and the canopy fading in.

### The options compared
**(a) Crowns as cut-outs of real looking-up photos, swayed by displacement and relit.** The most photoreal at the scale where photos win (clumps, limbs, real crown shyness), and measured at 0.5–0.8 ms. Its trouble is everything that changes: a photo is one moment of one tree. Autumn can be a recolouring of the photo's leaves (by luminance into a turned palette, in patches by a noise mask) and thinning a dissolve, but the dissolve shows sky where the real tree would show its limbs, since the limbs behind the leaves aren't in the photo. Bare winter crowns need other photos of other trees, so the stand changes identity at each season's swap, and a slow change through leaf fall can't be shown at all; only a swap at a rebuild (the app already rebuilds a wallpaper when the displays sleep after 12 hours) fits "never a hard cut". It needs openly licensed straight-up photos of each forest in at least summer, peak colour and bare, at 4,000 px or more and in flat light, matted against the sky; the look research found the best source: the zeniths of Poly Haven's forest HDRIs (CC0, 16–22K, linear light), straight-up views of beech and oak in leaf, beech turning, late autumn mostly bare, bare broadleaves beside snow-laden conifers, and a ring of spruce (research-look.md §1). A 16K panorama gives 45 px a degree at the zenith and a 22K one about 62, against the 26 this lens needs at the centre, 53 at the long sides' edges and 64 in the corners (derived): sharp in the middle, a little soft at the corners. Unchecked: how cleanly each was stitched at the zenith, where panoramas often blur or swirl. One lead fits (a)'s trouble exactly: NEON photographs the canopy straight up from the same points every 2–4 weeks from leaf-out to senescence at hardwood sites (Bartlett's beech, maple and birch; Harvard Forest; UNDERC's aspen and birch), CC BY 4.0, but as 180° fisheyes taken for measuring leaf area, behind a login; their quality as pictures is unchecked (§Data and licences).

**(b) Crowns built at load from scanned leaves into depth layers, swayed in one shader** (Dappled Light's method grown up; measured 0.36–0.66 ms). One bake holds every season: wood is always there, each spray carries an id, and the shader decides from the id and the date whether its leaves are unfolding, green, turning (to which of the scanned autumn colours), fallen, or under snow, so bud break, colour, thinning, bare lace and snow are one continuous change with no new picture. Light through the leaves is physical (§1): the light from above is the coverage of the layers farther up, read along the Sun's direction, so a crown's rim and the leaves under a gap glow and its core goes deep. Its risk is the one that cut A Tree for the Year: limbs and clumps laid out by code read as a drawn tree beside a physical sky. At 6–19 px a leaf, the leaves themselves are not the weak part; the structure is.

**(c) The hybrid, recommended: a real bare stand from a photo, with scanned leaves grown on its twigs at load.** A straight-up photo of a bare winter grove (the look research's candidates: Poly Haven's `snowy_forest_path_01` and `autumn_forest_04` zeniths, CC0; Commons' bare silver birch converging, CC BY 2.0, and bare crowns on overcast, public domain) (trunks, limbs and twigs against an overcast sky, which mattes almost by threshold) is the wood layer: real architecture, real crown shyness between its crowns, real convergence of its trunks, and evergreens where the stand has them (they stay as they are all year, as Dave asked). At load the bake finds the twigs (the matte's thin parts) and hangs sprays of scanned leaves on them in two or three depth layers, each spray with its id, so (b)'s seasons run on a real tree. The trunks are the photo's own, so no bark scans need matching to it; a looking-up photo has no "up", so each can be turned to any angle and mirrored, and with the leaves rolled afresh each load one photo gives many groves. What it needs that (b) doesn't: a tree-id map (which crown each pixel belongs to, flooded from each trunk along the matte) so each tree sways as a body about its own base rather than the picture wobbling like water, and the leaves sway with the wood they hang from (same displacement, read once). Cost as (b). Seasons as (b). Risks: the photo's lighting is baked into its bark (choose flat light; bark from below is in shade anyway), and leaves grown on twigs must fill crowns as densely as real ones (judge against a summer photo of a stand like it).

The three are compared in one mock-up sheet (§5), at the same moment and light, beside a real photo.

**Not:** a crown worked out per pixel (the Kelp probe measured that class at 4–9 ms); A Tree for the Year's 3D leaf bake (its lighting was right but its tree, as the subject, read drawn); a sprite a leaf (tens of thousands).

### Each season, in (b) and (c)
- **Leaf-out and green:** a spray's leaves appear on its own day within about two weeks after the species' bud break, small and yellow-green, darkening over the following weeks (Tree for the Year's sequence, in git at `ed079db^`).
- **Colour:** each spray turns within about ±2 weeks of the species' date, outer and upper crown first (the bake can rank sprays by depth into the crown), to a colour drawn from real scans of that species' autumn leaves: ambientCG's LeafSet027 and LeafSet028 (maples, green through orange to crimson, with translucency maps), LeafSet021 (small maple, yellow), LeafSet007 (yellow, birch- or elm-like), LeafSet008 and LeafSet011 (beech-like browns), LeafSet030 (oak, brown), all CC0 (checked, §Sources).
- **Leaf fall:** a spray drops when the season passes its id, sooner in a gust (its threshold lowered by the gust's strength), so the crown thins faster on a windy day and falling-leaf sprites are spawned in proportion (§2 has the rates). Beech and young oak keep a share of brown leaves through winter (Tree for the Year's marcescence).
- **Bare:** the wood alone, lace against the sky.
- **Snow on branches:** a white rim where the wood's matte is, read one pixel toward the sky's side and blurred (from below one sees the snow overhanging both edges of a limb, and twigs furred white), with conifers taking heavy masses; on when the snow load from the live weather (§2) says so.
- **Season setting:** holds one date's state (a day of the year), so the shader's one input is all it takes.

### What is baked, what runs live
- **Baked each load (or rebuild), off the main thread or on the GPU, 0.2–0.6 s:** the grove (photo, turn, mirror), the leaf layers with ids, their coverage at 1/8 for light from above, the tree-id map. Packed two layers to an RGBA texture: two full-resolution textures of the screen plus 10% are 29 MB each, the wood 29 MB, the small maps under 1 MB, so about 90 MB of texture at 3024 × 1964, or about 60 with the farther leaf layers at half resolution, which measured no dearer to draw (derived), in `sharedTexture` keyed by grove and size so a second display of the same size and Settings' live copy share it. A display of another size bakes its own.
- **Each minute, off the main thread:** the zenith sky (`SkyLight` with a zenith camera, about 20 ms, app) and the star sprites' places.
- **Each frame, on the CPU:** the wind (the live 10 m wind and gusts, smoothed), the odd falling leaf, and the canopy's few uniforms (time, wind, season, light). The canopy is one sprite, so a uniform set every frame marks one sprite, not thousands (Kelp's lesson). Per-tree sway phases come from the tree id hashed in the shader, so there are no per-tree uniforms (a shader takes about 30).
- **In the shader, by `u_now`:** sway (each tree's own frequency and phase from its id, its amplitude from the wind), branch sway, flutter (wrapped hourly), cloud drift.

**Battery.** With a crown 10 cm sway at 20 m being 7.6 px at 0.3 Hz, about 14 px/s at peak (derived), a calm day moves under 1.5 px a frame at 12 fps: `batteryRate` 12–15 by default, raised in wind (a `BatteryPaced` scene, as Night Sky's meteors and Dappled Light's light do), and lifted while a leaf is falling (a leaf leaving the screen moves 250–500 px/s, §4). Flutter at 2–5 Hz aliases below 10–12 fps, so it fades out as the rate drops. `batteryScale` gains little: the canopy is 0.36–0.66 ms and its 6–19 px leaves are its detail; measured, a 0.75 trim saves 0.15 ms of the heavy shader. Leave it out unless side-by-side shots say otherwise.

## 4. Risks, worst first
1. **The trees read drawn.** A Tree for the Year was cut because its tree, the subject, read as code and paper cards beside a photo and a physical sky. Here the crowns are the subject again. (c) puts a real stand's architecture under the leaves; (b) has only code's. Judge (a), (b) and (c) side by side with a real photo before anything else, at 100% as well as desktop size (Kelp: drawn things read drawn up close).
2. **Contrast: a dark lace on a bright sky.** The idea's own risk, and Kelp's looking-up view, which came out at a median of 192 and was greyed by its 0.42 exposure. It swings with the season: summer is mostly leaves with bright gaps, winter mostly sky with black lace, so the exposure must follow how much of the frame is sky (a partial auto-exposure over the canopy's coverage as well as the sky's light, as `SkyLight`'s is over the sky). Softeners, most effective first, as the brief ranks them: the light through the leaves (physics gives a sunlit leaf about the zenith's brightness, §1); small gaps held to the zenith's deep blue (in the model the zenith is a third to a half darker than the sky 30° toward the Sun, §1); a veiling glow where sky meets leaf (measured in the heavy shader); the Sun kept off the long axis and behind leaves. Winter's lace on an overcast white sky is the hardest picture: fine twigs against the deck should lighten with distance and blur by a pixel, as a camera sees them.
3. **Falling leaves come at you.** Looking straight up, anything that falls falls toward the camera: it hangs, grows, then streaks outward from the middle, faster the nearer it gets (a leaf 6 m off-axis falling 1 m/s leaves the screen at 250 px/s, one straight above at 3 m at 500, derived). That is the motion Dave turned down for rain and snow. Keep them few (§2's rates), let gusts carry them across rather than down, and fade or soften them out before they come nearer than about 5–6 m; a mock-up film has to judge it, with "none" as a variant.
4. **Sway that wobbles.** A smooth displacement field over the whole picture (Kelp's slabs, Dappled Light's shadows) reads as water or heat haze on a tree: crowns must move as bodies, each about its own base at its own frequency, limbs a little on their own, leaves fluttering in place. That needs the tree-id map and amplitudes from §2's numbers, not a noise.
5. **The Sun through moving leaves flashes.** In summer middays (§1) the Sun is in frame behind leaves; a gap opening and closing over it is a strobe. Its light and glare come from a blurred coverage map, changing over seconds; the disc is soft and never shows a star.
6. **Shimmer.** Leaves of 6–19 px sampled from a texture that moves by fractions of a pixel crawl at their edges. Pre-blur the bake by a texel (as Dappled Light does its canopy) and keep flutter a change of light, not of place.
7. **Seasons need dates for these trees.** `Phenology` is white oak's calendar only (app). Maple, beech, birch and aspen need their own offsets (§2), or the stand turns in one week where real woods turn over three or four.
8. **Kelp's crash and its uniforms.** Per-sprite shader attributes crashed the lock screen's still: falling leaves and trunks carry their data in their colour (`v_color_mix`), or have no shader. A uniform set marks every sprite using its shader: the canopy is one sprite; stars and leaves share none that change per frame. Pose nothing off the screen.
9. **Memory and load:** about 90 MB of texture at 2x and 0.2–0.6 s to bake (§3); a 6K display has 3.4 times the pixels (unmeasured). Settings' copy at its own size bakes again unless it borrows the desktop's.
10. **Photos:** Poly Haven's HDRIs are CC0 and Commons' mostly CC BY-SA, which NOTICE allows (credited, adaptations stay under it), each read at its source. An HDRI's zenith can carry a stitching blur or swirl; check each at 100% before building on it.

## 5. Mock-up sheets, and how to draw them honestly
**The renderer.** As Kelp Forest's and Jellyfish's: a standalone `docs/canopy/mock.swift` built with `swiftc -O`, drawing each tile with `SKRenderer` and the shaders meant for the build (this probe's canopy shader is the start), from a JSON of settings; `sheet.py` lays out sheets and films from `variants.py`, and `sheet.py stats` measures each tile's brightness at desktop size against the look research's caps. The sky from the app's own `Atmosphere`, copied in as this probe's `sky/main.swift` did, through a zenith camera, for a real date, time and place (42°N, Kelp's and Weather's fallback). Leaves from the ambientCG scans (colour and translucency maps, CC0), the photo stand for (a) and (c) from whichever photos the look research clears, stars from `stars.txt`. Each tile's GPU and CPU time printed beside it, so a pick is never a look the build can't afford. Delete rendered frames once the sheets are made.

**One question a sheet:**
1. **How the crowns are made:** (a) a summer photo cut out and relit; (b) all built from scans; (c) a real bare stand with scanned leaves grown on it; and the real photo the stand came from. Same place, date and light. The deciding sheet.
2. **Where you lie:** in a small gap, the crowns a ring round a channel of sky (sky about 30%); at a crown's edge (sky along one side); under closed canopy (sky 10% or less, in flecks); and 70°, 90° and 110° lenses on the first.
3. **The forest:** maple–beech–oak with two hemlocks or white pines in the ring; a birch grove with a spruce or two; an aspen grove; a mix. In summer and in peak colour.
4. **Softening the day:** the light through the leaves alone; plus the zenith's deep blue held in small gaps; plus the veiling glow; plus a lower exposure; the Sun behind leaves and the Sun in a gap. Brightness and contrast measured on each tile.
5. **The year in one stand:** six dates from the user's latitude (leaf-out, summer, colour starting, peak, half down, bare under snow), to judge each state and the colours drawn from the scans.
6. **Night:** a moonless dark site, a town's sky, winter's thin canopy with more stars, and the canopy lit faintly by the town against black leaves.
7. **Motion, as films:** sway in a calm, a breeze and a gusty day (the live wind's 5, 20 and 40 km/h); falling leaves at none, the real rate, and twice it, fading out at 10, 6 and 3 m.
