# Pixel Window Seat

The view from a window seat of an airliner that never lands, in [Pixel City](pixel-city.md)'s pixel art. The window fills most of the screen, with the dark cabin wall, the pale reveal and the lip of the shade round it. Outside, an endless land slides past, made up as we go and never the same twice: ocean with ships and their wakes, coasts with surf and shallows, cities on their grids of avenues, farmland in sections, forest, rivers and highways, mountains that stand up with snow on their crests, desert with its red rock and dunes, and country under snow. Cloud comes and goes with the land, from a clear sky through scattered cumulus and broken decks to a solid floor of cloud, and casts its shadows on the ground; far off, a storm now and then flickers with lightning. The Sun, Moon and stars are the real ones where you are, so the flight goes through your own dawn, day, sunset and night, when the cities turn to webs of orange and white lamps.

With Settings' **Earth orbit** on (0.105.0), the same window is on the International Space Station instead: the real Earth turns below at the station's own pace, an orbit every 93 minutes with its sunrise and its sunset, under the real Sun, Moon and stars, with a solar array wing where the airliner's wing was. That is a scene of its own, and has its own part of this page: [Earth orbit](#earth-orbit).

- **Files:** `Sources/Atrium/PixelWindowSeat.swift` holds all of it: the scene, the world (`SeatWorld`), its materials (`Mat`) and its noise. It uses Pixel City's canvas (`Pixels`, `RGB`, `mix`, `smoothstep`, `bayer`, `SeededRandom`) and two of its colour tables (`PixelCity.skyColours`, `PixelCity.sunColour`), `SkyMath.swift` for the Sun and Moon, and `Location.swift`. `Sources/Atrium/PixelOrbit.swift` holds the orbit's scene, `Sources/Atrium/Resources/orbit-earth.lzfse` its map, and `docs/pixel-window-seat/earth.py` bakes the map.
- **Entry:** `pixelWindowSeat(size:)` builds `final class PixelWindowSeat: SKScene`, or `PixelOrbit` while Earth orbit is on. Its entry in Scenes.swift is "Pixel Window Seat", icon `airplane`, tint `.blue`, with `PixelWindowSeat.knobs`.
- **Kind:** SpriteKit on Pixel City's low-resolution canvas, with no shader. The whole view through the pane is one picture, painted on the CPU up to 30 times a second into a mutable texture; the cabin is a second texture over it, repainted every 30 s.

## How it works

### The view
- **Canvas:** `pixel = max(2, round(height/240))` points per art pixel, as in Pixel City: 378 × 246 art pixels on a 1512 × 982 display.
- **The pane** is 90% of the canvas each way at Settings' Large, the default (60% at Small, 76% at Medium), kept between 8:5 (so a very wide display gets wall either side, not a letterbox) and 29:20 tall (a real window's shape, which a portrait display gets). On a 14-inch MacBook Pro it's 340 × 221 art pixels at Large and 287 × 186 at Medium. With Window set to None the pane is the whole canvas and no cabin is drawn. A real airliner window is taller than it is wide (0.71 to 0.73 wide for its height; 0.58 on a 787), so on a landscape screen this one is deliberately wider than life: see Dave's decisions.
- **The horizon** lies level, 62% of the way up the pane. Research on real footage found it 36 to 52% from the top with the camera level.
- **Every row of ground is one distance.** We look straight out of the side of the aircraft, so the ground slides sideways, parallel to the horizon, and a row of the picture `d` pixels below the horizon shows a strip of ground `cruise × focal / d` km away, where one pixel spans `cruise / d` km along the track. `focal` is 1.2 pane heights (a view about 45° tall and 65° wide). The nearest row is 20 km off at 90 m a pixel; the farthest is 310 km off at 1.4 km a pixel. The horizon is taken 4% of the pane's height above where the ground stops (`dip`), as the Earth's curve puts it, which also keeps the last rows from each spanning a thousand kilometres.
- **So motion is a strip sliding by whole pixels.** Each row keeps its strip of the world in a ring one pane wide (`land`, `glow`, `tall`, `cloud`). As the flight moves on, a row's strip slides on a pixel when the flight has covered that row's `across`: the near rows often, the far ones every few seconds. A pixel is worked out once, when it slides in at the leading edge, and keeps its place in the ring until it slides out. This is exactly how real footage moves (see Research): sideways, with speed rising in a straight line with the rows below the horizon, and nothing moving at the horizon itself.
- **The apron:** a sixth of the pane's height in extra rows nearer than its bottom edge shows (`apron`). Their level ground is out of sight below the pane, but a mountain or a cloud standing there rises into view and hides the foot of what is behind it. Without them the nearest mountains showed their whole near face as vertical bars along the bottom of the window.
- **The cloud layer is a second set of strips,** on a plane 2.4 km up, so 8.6 km below us: the same rows look at cloud 1.28 times nearer than the ground behind it, and it slides 1.28 times as fast. Real low cumulus measured 1.29 to 1.45 times the ground's speed.
- **The flight's place is shared** (`PixelWindowSeat.flown`, km along the track): every display's window and Settings' preview look out over the same country. It starts from the clock, so the flight has gone on while nobody watched, and moves on by scene time, so it steps the same at any frame rate and in the render tests.

### The world (`SeatWorld`)
Nothing is stored. What is at a spot (`x` km along the track, `z` km out from it) comes from hashes of the place: smooth noise (`noise`, one bump per unit) and layers of it (`fbm`).
- **Sea or land** (`shore`): four layers of noise 150 km across, with a fine crinkle for bays. Below zero is sea: deep, then paler toward the shore, then shallows, a broken line of surf and a strip of sand.
- **How built-up** (`urban`): noise 80 km across with some at 24 km. Above 0.6 is city, 0.5 to 0.6 suburbs, below that countryside; above 0.7 downtown begins.
- **Climate** (`climate`): two layers of noise 330 km across, from cold to hot. Snow lies where it's below about 0.37 and desert where it's above about 0.63, each thinning out in patches toward its edge (`snowy`, `sandy`, a finer noise added before the test), so the temperate middle always lies between the two and neither ends in a line.
- **Mountains** (`peaks`, `mountain`): ranges lie where a noise 170 km across runs high and the sea is well off. Within one the height is ridged noise: five layers, each `1 − |2n − 1|` squared, which puts a sharp crest along every line where the layer crosses its middle value and a valley between, up to about 3.5 km. A slope's tone comes from which way it faces: its height a little way east and north gives its tilt, and the light it takes is compared with what level ground takes, so it's one of three tones (facing the light, across it, turned away), or the darkest if a higher crest toward the Sun shades it (three samples, at 0.8, 2 and 4.5 km). What covers it goes by height: snow above the snowline (1.7 km in the temperate middle, down to the valley floor in the cold, all but absent in the desert), rock down to the treeline (1 km; red rock where it's desert, which has no trees), forest below, and meadows and a river on the valley floors. The far rows use only the first two or three layers and take the average of the rest.
- **Desert** (`desert`): sand, with dunes in wavering lines 1.1 km apart where a row is fine enough to show them, plateaux of red rock with a dark rim, the white of dry lake beds, a strip of fields either side of a river, a town in two of every ten 16 km squares, and in one section in fourteen an irrigated circle or a dark block of orchard. A city in the desert keeps its blocks and loses its green (`parched`).
- **Snow** (`frosted`): snow lies over whatever the lowland is. Fields and roofs go white, bare soil stays brown between them, woods keep their dark with clearings of snow, and roads and streets show as dark lines. A cold sea is nearly black, with pack ice along the shore breaking up into floes further out.
- **City:** avenues every 1.3 km with blocks between (parks are 7% of them), in districts of works with big pale roofs or of houses under trees. The avenues are drawn only where a block is at least 8 pixels wide or 4 rows deep; further off the blocks alone make the pattern, and beyond that the city is one tone. Downtown has towers, three pixels wide, standing up as columns (see Height).
- **Suburbs:** roofs thinning out among woods, with a main road every 2.6 km.
- **Countryside:** forest in irregular patches, and between them fields a 1.6 km section at a time, each section one field, two or four, each with its own crop. Where the land is dry (noise 140 km across) the crops turn to wheat, fallow and bare soil and some sections are irrigated circles. A town in six of every ten 16 km squares, a farm's yard light in some sections.
- **Rivers and highways** (`onLine`) are the lines along which a noise crosses its middle value, drawn a constant width by dividing by how fast the noise changes there. So they wind on for ever, through town and country alike, without being stored.
- **Ships:** one in three of the 11 km squares of open sea has a ship, a pale dash with a wake six times its length.
- **Things too small to see are left out.** Every lookup is told how many km the pixel spans (`across`, `deep`): field patterns stop where a row spans more than 1.3 km, towers and whitecaps where it spans more than about half a km, and so on. Without this the far rows turn to noise.
- **Cloud** (`cover`, `cloudTop`, `cloud`): how much of the sky below is cloud is itself part of the world (two layers of noise 260 km across), so we fly into and out of weather, and a new deck shows first as a line under the horizon and takes minutes to arrive. Where there is cover, clouds are the places where puffy noise (3.4 km across, clumped at 15 km) rises above a threshold that falls as the cover rises: a few separate cumulus, then clumps, then a deck with holes, then a solid floor. Seen nearly edge-on, far cloud hides its own gaps, so the threshold also falls with distance. A cloud's byte holds its height (an index into `SeatWorld.heights`, 0 to 6.5 km in steps that are fine for small clouds), how thick and so how bright its top is, and whether a taller cloud toward the Sun shades it.
- **Shadows:** a patch of ground is in shadow if there is cloud where the Sun's light would come through the cloud layer: `(deck + 0.4) / tan(elevation)` km toward the Sun. It's worked out as the pixel slides in, with the Sun where it is then. No shadows below 5° of Sun.
- **Storms** (`storm`, `SeatWorld.lightning`): a storm is one of the tower clouds' own bumps of noise, where the cover is thick: half of those, about 4 in every 100 corners of the towers' 30 km grid. A pixel of cloud within 9 km of a storm's heart carries how near it is and which of sixteen rhythms the storm keeps. Each rhythm beats every 6 to 13 s: three beats in four hold one burst of 0.1 to 0.9 s, flickering every 0.08 s, and the rest pass in quiet, so a storm is lit about 3% of the time.
- **Scenery and Clouds in Settings** lean these fields: Ocean leaves only islands; the rest leave no sea. City and Countryside push `urban` up or down, have no mountains and keep the climate temperate; Mountains makes nearly everywhere a range, in whatever climate comes; Desert and Snow fix the climate and thin the cities out. Clouds fixes the cover.

### The picture (`compose`)
- **Sky** first, copied from a buffer the last repaint made.
- **Ground,** nearest row first. Each pixel's colour is its material at one of 12 steps of haze, the row's haze stepped between two bands with Pixel City's 4×4 dither, in sunlight or in a cloud's shadow. Water under the Sun sparkles (the glitter path) and lamps come on after dark. Land that stands up (a mountain, a tower downtown) paints a column from its own row up to its top, and each column of the picture remembers how high the ground in front already reaches (`ceiling`), so what lies behind a ridge stays hidden and nothing is painted twice.
- **Cloud,** the same way with a ceiling of its own: a cloud stands up from its base as a column, and one behind paints only what shows above those in front. Its top takes one of six tones: brighter where it's thick and where it faces the Sun's side, two tones darker in another cloud's shadow. Its foot and waist, which show only where nothing nearer hides them, are the shaded face. A pixel inside a storm is blended toward lightning's blue-white by how near the storm's heart it is and how brightly its rhythm is lit at that moment.
- **Cloud always paints over ground.** That is right so long as the mountains are lower than the cloud's base (2.4 km): a ray from 11 km to a cloud passes above any nearer thing lower than the cloud. Most crests are; where one stands higher, cloud beyond it is drawn across its top, which reads as cloud sitting on the peak.
- **The other airliner,** then **the wing** and its lights.
- **How often:** as often as the fastest strip (the cloud in the nearest row) moves a pixel and half as often again, between 15 and 30 times a second (`paintEvery`): with the Large window that is 15 times a second at the standard 1× and up to 1.9×, and 30 from 3.8×. Painting is most of the scene's cost. `redraw` paints too (see Gotchas).

### Height
A thing `h` km tall at a row stands `h × d / cruise` pixels up the picture (`Row.lift`), since raising a point is the same as lowering the camera toward it: 11 pixels a km at the nearest row. `tall` holds it in steps of 20 m. Mountains are drawn at life size; towers half as tall again (`surface` stores them so); clouds at life size, over their own plane.

### The light (`redraw`, every 30 s by the wall clock)
- **Sun and Moon** from `Sky` at `Location.shared`, the Moon with its real phase. They are placed as Pixel City places them: 250° of compass across the pane, and elevation as the 0.75 power of elevation / 60°, so they stay in view. That is a liberty: a true 65° view would show the Sun only when it's low and nearly straight out of the window.
- **Sky:** `PixelCity.skyColours`, banded and dithered the same way, with the zenith 22% darker by day (there is less air above us), the horizon orange under a low Sun and lilac opposite it, a couple of hundred seeded stars, the Sun's glare, and the Moon.
- **Ground:** each material's colour times the sky's light plus the Sun's (`PixelCity.sunColour`, by the sine of its elevation), then 20% of a blue veil, since 11 km of air lie in front of even the nearest ground, then haze toward the horizon's colour with distance: 10% at the nearest row, 44% at 60 km, 81% at 150 km. At night the ground is nearly black, and moonlight adds a little by how much of the Moon is lit.
- **Cloud** is lit by the Sun as it stands 4° higher than it does for the ground, so the tops stay lit after the land has gone dark, and never goes quite black.
- **The wing** is lit as the Sun stands 3.4° higher: the Sun still reaches an aircraft at 11 km when it's that far below the horizon of the ground beneath it.
- **Lamps** come on through dusk, each when the evening passes its own random draw, so a city doesn't light all at once; about 3% of them are out for two thirds of a second at a time, as lamps seen from a moving aircraft do behind trees and roofs. Avenues are continuous lines of alternate bright and dim lamps; districts are sodium orange or the white of streets since relit (noise 11 km across).
- **The glitter path:** sparkles scattered on water under the Sun's place, reshuffled three times a second, denser near the Sun's image. Under the Moon it's the same and far fainter.

### The cabin and the wing
- **The cabin** (`paintCabin`) is dark, as a camera set for the daylight outside sees it: the wall, a dark seal round the pane, a reveal of pale plastic nine pixels wide whose lower lip takes the daylight (and the sunset's colour) and whose upper lip is in its own shadow, the shade's lip across the top 9% of the pane with its tab, seams in the wall panels, and the breather hole at the foot of the pane with a few specks of frost. The pane's outline is a rounded rectangle (`outsidePane`).
- **The wing** (`layWing`, the seat over it) is a list of the pane's pixels it covers, marked out once from two polygons: the wing running from under the window out to its tip just below the horizon, and the winglet above it. We look out of the left side, so the nose is to the right, the land slides right to left, and the wing sweeps back to the left. Its leading edge is bright metal, with a panel line and darker flaps behind.
- **Its lights:** a red light at the tip after dark (the left wing's colour), and a white strobe for a tenth of a second every 1.3 s (46 a minute; the rule is 40 to 100), which at night catches the few pixels of metal round it.
- **The other airliner:** every one to four minutes one crosses the sky far off at 5 pixels a second, three pixels long with 110 pixels of contrail thinning out behind it. At night it's a red light, a white flash and a ghost of the trail.

## Time, live data and appearance
- **Time:** the real time, or today at the preview hour.
- **Location:** `Location.shared`, for the Sun and Moon. `Location.shared.start()` is called in `didMove`.
- **Network:** none for the airliner. In orbit, only while one of its two switches is on (see [Earth orbit](#earth-orbit)).
- **Appearance:** it ignores Light and Dark Mode. The real Sun already sets the look.

## Settings
`PixelWindowSeat.knobs`. A change repaints at once. A change of Scenery or Clouds is a different world, and a change of Window a different size of picture, so each hands over to a new scene through a 0.8 s crossfade with both still running, as Pixel City does for a change of city; with no view to fade in, as in the render tests, a change of world lays every row again and a change of window waits for the next scene.
- **Earth orbit** (`seat.orbit`, off by default): the space station in place of the airliner. While it's on, Seat, Looking, Scenery and the time preview below are hidden (`Knob.hiddenWhen`) and the orbit's own Seat, Looking and two switches show in their place ([its settings](#its-settings)); Window, Speed, Clouds and Lightning serve both.
- **Seat** (`seat.wing`): Over the wing (the default) or Ahead of the wing, which has nothing but the view.
- **Window** (`seat.window`): Small, Medium, Large (the default), or None, just the view: no cabin, and the view fills the screen. The wing still goes by Seat.
- **Looking** (`seat.looking`): Toward the midday Sun (the default: toward the equator, so the Sun and Moon cross the window) or one of eight compass points. Face west and the Sun sets in the window.
- **Speed** (`seat.speed`, 0.5× to 10×, 1× by default): 1× is a real airliner, 250 m a second, whose nearest ground takes over a minute and a half to cross the window. Scenery and weather change faster with it too, since both are part of the country we fly over.
- **Scenery** (`seat.scenery`): Changing, or held over Ocean (with the odd island), City, Countryside, Mountains, Desert or Snow.
- **Clouds** (`seat.clouds`): Changing, Clear, Scattered, Broken or Overcast.
- **Lightning in far-off storms** (`seat.lightning`, on by default).
- **Preview a time of day** (`seat.previewTime`, `seat.previewHour`): shows today at that hour instead of now.

## Tuning constants
- **Flight:** `cruise` 11 km, `deck` 2.4 km, `pace` 0.25 km/s.
- **View:** pane 76% of the canvas, 8:5 at widest; horizon at 62%; `focal` 1.2 pane heights; `dip` 4%.
- **Haze:** `0.1 + 0.9 × (1 − e^(−(away − 19) / 85))`, in 12 steps; the veil is 20%.
- **How the scenery falls out,** measured over 60,000 km of track 30 km out from it (the middle of the near ground): ocean 27%, city and suburbs 24%, countryside 15%, mountains 15%, snow 10%, desert 8% (those last two counting only lowland that is neither city nor mountain: a city under snow counts as city). A stretch of ocean is 79 km long at the median and 218 km at the ninetieth percentile; city 31 and 116; countryside 17 and 102; mountains 28 and 149; desert 27 and 111; snow 15 and 104. At the standard 1× that's 15 km a minute: the country changes about every one to five minutes, and nine stretches in ten of any kind are over within fifteen (ocean is the long one; the rest are over within ten). At 3×, the default until 0.104.1, it was a third of that.
- **Mountains:** ranges where the 170 km noise passes 0.64 (full by 0.8); heights `(ridged − 0.1) × 4` km; the snowline 1.7 km and the treeline 1 km in the temperate middle.
- **Cloud cover** along the same track: clear 35% of the way, scattered 30%, broken 22%, overcast 13%.
- **Lamps** (`Mat.lamps`): dim and full sodium, a bright one for avenues, warm white, cool white.

## Performance
Release build, 2x, 1512 × 982, measured 2026-10-10 with the render test (which steps 30 frames a second) at the standard Large window and 1×:
- **Per frame:** 0.77 to 1.18 ms CPU and 0.12 to 0.38 ms GPU across the fourteen screenshots, each measured twice (two shots read 0.51 and 0.82 ms GPU once, and 0.12 and 0.14 the other time). The most CPU is under a deck of cloud (1.03 to 1.18) and over the storm at night (1.08), then with no window (0.93). Nearly all of it is painting the picture and copying it into the texture, which at 1× happens on every other frame of the test's. With the Medium window at 3×, the defaults until 0.104.1, it was 0.68 to 0.96 ms CPU.
- **Painting every frame** (as it did before 0.104.0, and still does from about 3.8× up with the Large window, 4.5× with Medium) costs about 1.0 ms with the Medium window over mountains, 1.3 ms with Large and 1.4 to 1.8 ms with none: the picture is 53,000 art pixels at Medium, 75,000 at Large and 93,000 with no window.
- **New pixels** cost almost nothing: a few hundred a second across all rows. A pixel of mountain is the dearest, at some forty noise lookups (its own height, its tilt, and the crests that might shade it).
- **A new scene** lays every row: about 59,000 ground pixels at Large (42,000 at Medium) and as many of cloud.
- **Debug builds** paint at 10 to 30 ms a frame. Measure in release.

## Gotchas and shortcuts
- **`rgb(_:_:_:)` is private here and in PixelCity.swift, on purpose.** When it was shared, `rgb(1, 1, 1)` in CampfireFlames.swift quietly became this one (whole numbers prefer it) in place of Fireflies' `rgb` of CGFloats that makes a CGColor, a gradient came back nil and the full test run crashed. The other canvas helpers have no namesakes.
- **Sideways only.** The row trick needs the view square to the track and the horizon level: no bank, no looking ahead. A view turned toward the nose would move every pixel on a curve and need the whole picture worked out each frame.
- **Tower clouds stand only in the far rows** (a `ponytail:` in `cloudTop`: `towering` fades out where a row spans less than 1.6 km). Up close a column of one tone reads as a block of ice, not a cloud: the first try had them at any distance, 100 pixels tall. A near one would need its own lumpy drawing.
- **Cloud edges ramp up over a wider margin than they first did** (`min(t × 4, 1)`), and decks are no more than a kilometre thick: with sheer edges, a hole in a deck showed its far wall as a cliff.
- **Shadows keep the Sun they slid in with** (a `ponytail:` in `ground`). A far row can show shadows cast for a Sun several minutes old. They are too small there to tell.
- **Lamps are denser than life.** Research found only 1.5 to 3% of pixels lit in real night photographs of cities. Ours run 10 to 30% in the blocks, which is what reads as a city at this size; it was three times that at first, an orange carpet.
- **The Sun's and Moon's places are stretched** to keep them in the window (see The light). The glitter path follows the stretched place.
- **The strobe is a few pixels.** It first lit a disc of wing 14 pixels across, which the screenshots caught as a pale circle on the wing. Check anything that flashes on its flash frame.
- **The far airliner's and the lightning's clocks are the scene's own,** counted from when it was built, so two displays don't flash together, and a screenshot can catch a flash again (`SNAPSHOT_SECONDS=8.02` over the storm in `-storm`).
- **A boundary drawn where a smooth field crosses a value is a straight line** at this scale. Desert rock met grey rock along a ruler's edge until the test took `sandy`'s patchy version of the climate; any new either-or by climate needs the same.
- **Per-pixel random specks read as television static** on anything dark: snow in the woods was 30% white specks at first. It's clearings from a 1.2 km noise now.
- **The Large window sits centred,** not 3 pixels up like the others: its surround is 12 pixels and the margin only 12.
- **Cloud is drawn over any mountain** (see The picture): right for crests below 2.4 km, a liberty above.
- **`redraw` paints the picture itself,** as well as `update`. A view that is frozen (Low Power Mode) or not drawing yet (Settings' preview as its page opens) never reaches `update`, and the first build, which painted only there, showed an empty grey pane behind Settings' header. Found with `SETTINGS_SHOT`; fixed in 0.103.1.
- **A mutable texture's rows run from the bottom,** as `picture`'s do; `Pixels.bytes(topDown: false)` gives the sky the same way.
- **zsh and `$at:seat…`:** in a shell loop, write `${at}:seat.previewHour`, or zsh reads `:s` as a modifier.

## Earth orbit

Settings' Earth orbit swaps the airliner for the International Space Station: the same pane, set in a module's wall, looking out from 420 km over the real Earth as it turns below. An orbit takes 93 minutes: about 46 over daylit ground, an orbital sunset, 36 in the Earth's shadow over city lights, moonlit cloud and the airglow, and a sunrise. New in 0.105.0. From 0.106.0 it can also look from the station's Cupola, its dome of seven windows: down into the dome from the module above it, or from inside the dome through a fisheye, with the whole Earth as a disc behind the panes ([The Cupola](#the-cupola-pixelcupola)).

- **Files:** `Sources/Atrium/PixelOrbit.swift` holds the scene (`PixelOrbit`), the orbit (`Orbit`), the map (`EarthMap`), the weather and lamps (`OrbitWorld`) and the colours (`OrbitPaint`). `Sources/Atrium/PixelCupola.swift` holds the Cupola's dome (`PixelCupola`). `Sources/Atrium/Resources/orbit-earth.lzfse` is the map, baked by `docs/pixel-window-seat/earth.py`. It borrows the airliner's pane (`PixelWindowSeat.pane`), its materials' colours (`Mat.day`) and its lightning (`SeatWorld.lightning`), Pixel City's canvas, `SkyMath.swift`, Live Sky's star catalogue (`LiveSky.catalogue`), `ISS.swift` and `Clouds.swift`.
- **Entry:** `pixelWindowSeat(size:)` builds `PixelOrbit` while `seat.orbit` is on. Turning it on or off, like any of the orbit's settings but Speed, dissolves into a new scene over 0.8 s with both still running.
- **Kind:** as the airliner: one picture painted on the CPU into a mutable texture 15 to 30 times a second, under a second texture for the window's frame. From the Cupola there is no second texture: the dome is painted into the one picture.

### The view
- **The pane and the lens are the airliner's:** `PixelWindowSeat.pane(_:)` lays out both, so the two dissolve into each other pane for pane, and the lens (`OrbitLens`, which is the one place a pixel becomes a ray and a direction a pixel) has a `focal` of 1.2 pane heights, 45° tall and 65° wide (a 28 mm lens on the station's own cameras). It can also be an equidistant fisheye, which From the Cupola uses.
- **Three ways to look** through that pane (`looks`, Settings' Looking), and two more from the Cupola, which has no pane and [its own part](#the-cupola-pixelcupola) below:
  - **Out the side:** to port, as the airliner looks out of its left side, with the way we fly to the right and the ground sliding right to left, tipped 25° down. That puts the Earth's edge 38% down the pane, where the airliner's horizon is. Photographs from the station put it 10 to 45% down, mostly 20 to 35%.
  - **Ahead:** the same tip, along the track. Land comes up off the limb and spreads toward us.
  - **Straight down:** with the way we fly to the right.
- **From 420 km** the Earth's edge is 20° below level and 2,350 km away, and it's a circle: across the pane it bows about 20 pixels. Out of the side the nearest ground in the pane is 570 km off at 2.1 km a pixel, and the last row under the limb is 7.6 km a pixel across and over 100 km deep. Straight down a pixel is 1.6 km. The point under the station is never in the side view.
- **The pace at 1×:** the ground under the station passes at 7.2 km/s. Out of the side the bottom row crosses the pane in 94 s and the row at the limb in over six minutes (the limb is not at infinity, so it moves too); straight down a cloud crosses in about 75 s.

### How the ground moves (`lay`, `advance`)
The Earth turns under the station about the orbit's pole, so a spot of ground keeps its distance to one side of the track and moves along a parallel of the orbit. The airliner's rows become strips between such parallels, which cross the pane as arcs.
- **`lay`, once:** every pixel gets its ray. A pixel that meets the ground gets its two angles on the Earth (how far round the orbit from the point under us, and how far to port of the track), and from its four neighbours how much of each one pixel spans.
- **Strips:** walking from the least angle to port in view to the greatest, each strip is as wide as the finest pixel that looks at that part of the Earth (found in 1,024 bins).
- **Cells and rings:** a strip is cut into cells as long as its finest pixel. Pixels that span twice, four times, up to 128 times that length use a ring of cells that size instead (`level`), so every pixel shows about one cell. A `Ring` is one strip at one size of cell: where on the Earth it lies, how long its cells are, and the stretch of it the pane looks at. Each pixel keeps its ring and its place in it (`ringOf`, `place`).
- **That is what serves every view.** Out of the side and straight down, nearly every pixel of a strip is at its finest size, and the picture slides by whole pixels as the airliner's does. Looking ahead, a strip runs from the pane's foot to the limb and passes through every size.
- **`advance`, each paint:** a ring's first cell is where the station has got to, in cells; cells that have come into view are laid, each once (`OrbitWorld.cell`). Cell `n` of a ring lies `n` cell-lengths round the orbit from where it crosses the equator northward, and its place on the Earth comes from the orbit's own directions and the Earth's turn at that moment (`Orbit.lands`).
- **Counts** on a 14-inch MacBook Pro with the Large window: out of the side 244 rings and 49,244 cells; straight down 224 and 77,132; ahead 3,059 and 53,528. Into the Cupola 191 and 27,563, for the 15,532 pixels the dome leaves as sky; From the Cupola 739 and 34,799, for 14,740 pixels of ground (its other 7,028 of sky are the air along the limb and space beyond it).

### The orbit (`Orbit`)
- **A circle** 420 km up, tilted 51.63°, with two rates: 5,579.3° a day round it (92.9 minutes from one northward crossing of the equator to the next) and 4.95° a day for the crossing's slide westward among the stars.
- **Set going from one real fix of the ISS:** 10 October 2026, 15:43:18 UTC, over 22.20° S 174.85° E heading north, from api.wheretheiss.at. That put it 331.37° round its orbit with the crossing at right ascension 88.67°. The rates are from the same feed's elements for that day (15.48793 orbits a day, plus the 3.7° a day the orbit's own long axis swings round). A second fix 603 s later came out within 0.04° of latitude and 0.012° of longitude, which `orbitKeepsToTheRealStation` keeps true.
- **Shared and stepped as the airliner's flight is:** `PixelOrbit.orbit` is one for every copy of the scene, starts from the clock at launch and moves on by scene time times Speed. The moment it's at (`date`) moves on with it, so at 10× the Earth turns ten times as fast too, the track still shifts 23° west each orbit, and the Sun is where it would be then.
- **Follow the real ISS:** while it's on the scene flies at the real pace whatever Speed was left at (`pace`), and Settings greys the Speed slider out at 1× with a small lock beside it (`Knob.lockedWhen`); the stored speed comes back when it's turned off. A fix from `ISS.shared` (the feed Earth from Orbit and Live Sky already poll, here once a minute from an action started in `didMove`) is turned into an orbit through that place (`Orbit(date:latitude:longitude:climbing:)`). If it's far from where we are (0.57° round the orbit or of the crossing, or 5 s of time) the orbit jumps there, and every copy of the scene notices that the station has moved further than a frame's worth (`reached`) and dissolves into a new scene. After that each fix is a nudge of a twentieth of the difference. Fixes above 45° of latitude are skipped: near the top of the orbit a hair of latitude is a long way round it.

### The Earth (`EarthMap`, `OrbitWorld.cell`)
- **The map** is one byte for every 4.9 km between 73° north and south (the station never sees past 72°): 8192 × 3322, 3.0 MB packed with LZFSE, 27 MB in memory, and held only while an orbit scene is alive (a weak reference). The low five bits are the ground's material, the high three how brightly it is lit at night.
- **Baked** by `docs/pixel-window-seat/earth.py` from NASA's Blue Marble Next Generation for September 2004 with topography and bathymetry (21600 × 10800) and its Black Marble for 2016 (13500 × 6750), both reduced to 8192 wide first. Water is whatever is clearly blue, in four depths by how dark the bathymetry shades it. Land takes the material of the nearest of eighteen colours sampled from the map at known places (the Libyan desert, the Sahel, the Congo, the taiga, Greenland and so on).
- **Eighteen materials,** in the airliner's own colours by name (`OrbitPaint.day` from `Mat.day`): deep water, sea, a shelf sea (the one new colour), shallows, sand, dune, ochre sand, red rock, steppe, scrub, pasture, grass, woods, pine, forest, rock, old snow and snow.
- **A far cell,** longer or wider than 9 km, takes the commonest material of five places across it and the brightest lamp, so a far coast doesn't flicker as cells step past it.
- **Lamps:** a cell is lit by chance, one in seven where the night map is dimmest and three in five in a city's heart: sodium orange, dim or full, a bright one here and there, white downtown, and whole regions in cool white (noise 700 km across) for cities since relit. They show where the ground's light is below the fifth of its sixteen steps, and 3% of them are out for a moment at a time, as the airliner's are.

### The weather (`OrbitWorld.cloud`)
- **Made up by default:** systems from noise 2,600, 1,000 and 380 km across, bent by noise 1,500 km across so they curl, and drifting 8° west a day. The cover is kept near the half at which cloud begins (`0.5 + (noise − 0.54) × 1.3`), so most of a system is a broken field and only its heart a deck.
- **Clumps and puffs** break it up: noise 70, 26, 11 and 5 km across, each only where a cell's longer side is under about four fifths of it (56, 21, 9 and 4 km). A cell too big to show a puff would take it at one place along that side, and the far ground, where a cell is some 30 by 150 km, was one-pixel speckle for it, worst along the limb. So far cloud is smooth masses, and a far pixel flickers about half as often as the ground slides (0.105.2; until then only the two finest were held back, and by the cell's shorter side). Cloud is where cover plus puff passes a half, in three tones by how far past.
- **How much:** cloud over 34% of the Earth; of 237 stretches of track 1,500 km long, 9 under a solid deck and 73 clear (`orbitWeatherLeavesTheGroundInSight`). The first try had weather either clear or overcast, and a whole window of white for minutes at a time.
- **Settings' Clouds** leans it: Clear has none, Scattered keeps the cover under the half, Broken holds it near the half everywhere, Overcast is a deck.
- **Today's real clouds:** the cover comes from `Clouds.shared.cover` instead, the live map Earth from Orbit shows, with the same puffs over it. Its small copy is 78 km a pixel, so it's smoothed between its pixels (`OrbitWorld.live`; taken one at a time they showed as squares 30 pixels wide), and it gives the systems their real places and no more.
- **Shadows:** a cell of open ground is in shadow if there is cloud where the Sun's light would come through a deck 6 km up. Cloud itself is flat: from orbit a 10 km tower stands 1 to 3 pixels, and slides against the ground behind it by only 3 to 6% (see Research), so there is no second layer of strips as the airliner has.
- **Storms:** in thick cloud where noise 300 km across runs high, with one of fifteen of the airliner's rhythms of lightning by the place.

### The picture (`compose`)
- **Ground:** each pixel's cell, in the colour of its material at one of sixteen steps of light and twelve of haze, in the open or in a cloud's shadow. The light is the Sun's height over that spot of ground (the cosine of its angle from overhead: −0.16, −0.09, −0.04, 0, 0.05, 0.13, 0.3 and 0.6 for the eight tones, with a step half way between each pair), dithered between steps, so the terminator is a band across the picture: night, blue twilight, red, orange, warm, day.
- **Night is lifted** (Dave, see below): a moonlit one, in which sea, land and cloud can be told apart, a quarter brighter again under a full Moon and a quarter dimmer under a new one. A true one is black.
- **Haze** by how slantwise we look through the air: a tenth of full straight down, most of it at the limb, toward the haze's own colour at that step of light.
- **Cloud** takes the light two steps later than the ground under it: the tops stay lit after the sea has gone dark (measured: 20 to 40 s).
- **The Sun's glint:** sparkles on water where the sea would mirror the Sun to us, thickest at its image, reshuffled three times a second.
- **The air along the limb:** pixels whose ray misses the Earth by less than 126 km are coloured by how high it passes, in fourteen bands of 9 km (a pixel each at the limb's distance), from three ramps taken from photographs (see Research): by day pale at the ground and blue above, gone by 58 km; through sunrise and sunset dark at the bottom, then red, orange and cream, white at 45 km, blue above; by night the green line of the airglow 96 km up over a dull orange layer, with a dark gap under it. The air is lit from further round the Earth than the ground under it, since we see it from the side.
- **Stars** are the real ones (the Yale catalogue Live Sky uses), turning about the station as it goes round. They are dimmed to 15% while the station is in sunlight, which leaves a handful: a camera set for the sunlit Earth sees none.
- **The Moon,** by how much of it is lit, and **the Sun,** with a glare that is wide and warm while it's on the limb. Out of the side the Sun's bearing is squeezed to three tenths, as the airliner squeezes its Sun's, so that it rises at the right of the window and sets at the left: the real station flies toward its sunrise, and a true side view would show the disc only a few days a year. The light on the ground stays true.

### The wing and the window (`layWing`, `paintFrame`)
- **The wing** is one solar array wing: two blankets either side of a lattice mast, 35 m long and 10 across, lying flat below the window from its lower left corner out toward the limb, where the airliner's wing is (straight down, it lies across the lower right corner). It's marked out once, by where each pixel's ray meets its plane: blanket, a fold line every 2.3 m, a seam along each blanket, a lit rim, and the mast's lattice.
- **Two faces:** its dark cells with a pale grid one day and its gold back the next. Which is up changes at midnight, when either is the same dark shape. Each has its colours in the Sun, in the low Sun (warmer, for the minutes the Sun is within 7° of the limb) and in the Earth's shadow, blended by how much of the Sun shows over the limb from the station, which is lit five minutes before the ground under it and five after.
- **The window's frame:** a dark seal, one thin bright line where the bezel catches the light, a ring of metal with a bolt every 18 pixels along its straight sides, and the wall beyond with its panel seams, dark as a camera set for the Earth sees it. Painted once.

### The Cupola (`PixelCupola`)
Two more choices of Looking (0.106.0), from the dome of seven windows on the station's Earth-facing side: a round one with six trapezium panes round it. Both look straight down with the way we fly to the right, as Straight down does, and both have the whole canvas for their pane, as Window's None does. The research behind every number and colour is under [Research](#the-cupola), and Dave's picks under his decisions.
- **Into the Cupola** (`looks` 3): floating in Tranquility, the module the dome hangs from, with the eye 2.47 m from the round window (where the fit to S130-E-009694 put the photographer), through a rectilinear lens 45° tall. On a laptop's canvas (378 × 246) the round window is 84 pixels across, 82 of them sky, and the seven openings 212 pixels tall. A pixel of ground is 1.4 km. Only ground shows in the panes: the limb is out of sight.
- **From the Cupola** (`looks` 4): head in the dome, the eye 0.45 m from the round window, through the equidistant fisheye, a half turn across the canvas's shorter side. The round window is 104 pixels across, 90 of them sky, and the openings 228 tall. The Earth is a disc 191 pixels across whose limb runs through the six side panes, with the air, space, the stars, the Sun and the Moon beyond it. A pixel of ground at the middle is 5.4 km.
- **The lens is over the canvas's shorter side** (`PixelCupola.init`), where the other views' is over the pane's height, so on a portrait display the whole flower of panes still fits across (the round window 47 and 57 pixels across at 1080 × 1920). The 45° is exact, 1.2071 sides, as the mock-ups' lens was; the other views' 1.2 is 45.2°.
- **The model.** Everything is a shape in the dome's own axes, in metres from the round window's centre: x the way we fly, y to starboard, z toward the Earth. The eye is on the axis at z = −2.47 or −0.45. `PixelCupola.window(_:depth:grow:)` is a window's outline: the round one a circle of radius 0.353, a side pane a trapezium (short edge 0.400, long edge 0.644, 0.405 tall, corners of 0.0324 radius) whose short edge is 0.505 from the axis at z = −0.016 and which leans 28.4° from the axis, turned 60° at a time. `spot` puts a point through the scene's own lens (`OrbitLens.place`, the lens's `spot` without the rounding), `fill` marks the pixels whose centres are inside an outline (even-odd, a row at a time), `trace` walks a line, and `grow` and `shrink` add or take off a pixel round a shape. Nothing is placed in pixels but what is a pixel thick: a line one pixel out from a shape, a bolt every four pixels along a line.
- **What looks out** (`sky`). A window is open from its outline at the room to a second copy of it 0.11 m further out along its normal (0.10 for the round one), where the glass is: a pixel is sky where it is inside both, and the ridged wall of the recess where it is inside the first only. `PixelOrbit.lay` gives a ring to no pixel the dome covers, so no ground is laid that nobody sees.
- **The inside** (`paint`), by day, in the order it is painted: the dome's wall (#0b0c10); the black plate the windows are set in (a circle of 0.80 at z = −0.42, #16181d) with the base ring's lip round it; twelve wall panels, barely lit; the long blue rail round the base in six lengths with silver ends; two braided hoses; aft, a glossy monitor with the Sun's glare in it; forward to port a keypad's box with four keys; a crew patch with a red ring; the hand controller in its guard; a row of silver bolts round every window, one every four pixels; two tan bags strapped between the round window and the panes to port, lit from the upper left; eight white labels; then each window's recess in two greys, its silver bezel and, next the opening, its copper line; twelve orange tags; a short blue handle with silver ends along each of the six bars; and, beyond the dome, the hatch's grey ring and Tranquility's dark (from within the dome that is behind the eye, and shows only in the picture's corners).
- **The hardware outside** (`layHardware`), for the seat By the solar array: the round window's own shutter, a drum 0.42 m in radius on an arm beyond the bar to starboard; a Soyuz close by and a Progress further off, hanging toward the Earth aft, each four cylinders and two gold panels; and the tip of a gold array forward and to port. A cylinder is three tones, shaded, plain and lit, from a Sun that never moves (above and a little ahead and to port). It is laid once, and only where a window shows it. Into the dome only the drum shows, in the two panes to starboard: the ships are off the picture and the array reaches behind the 45° lens, where it is left out. From within, all of it shows.
- **The light** (the end of `compose`). The hardware is lit as the wing is, each pixel with its colour in the Sun, the same warmed (× 1.0, 0.80, 0.62) for a low Sun and a dark silhouette in the Earth's shadow, with a faint rim where nothing stands between it and the middle of the picture. Then the dome goes over everything, the stars, the Sun's glare and the hardware included: its day painting while the station is in sunlight, and the same painting times (0.30, 0.35, 0.46) in the Earth's shadow, blended by how much of the Sun shows over the limb from the station (`lit`). In shadow the monitor and the keypad each keep one pixel of standby light, green and amber. While the Sun is within 7° of the limb (`low`), the bezel on the far side of each window from the Sun takes a warm glint (#e9b06c): the Sun's side is its real bearing in the picture.
- **True to the station:** the windows' sizes (ESA's); their lean, their distance from the axis and the depth of the recess (fitted to one photograph, checked on a second); the colours (measured in the photographs); what is on the plate and round the base, and roughly where; the shutter's drum beyond a bar; the Russian ships aft and a port array forward.
- **Not true to it:**
  - **The dome is turned 30°.** The real one has a bar dead ahead and dead aft. Ours has a pane there (Dave's pick), so the Sun rises in a pane when it rises ahead. Everything outside is placed by the turned dome.
  - **The hardware's distances are chosen,** so that each piece shows at a pane's outer part: the Progress 30 m off and the Soyuz 15, the array 30. Their sizes are real.
  - **The shutter is folded back 12°** past flat, so that it shows from both eyes.
  - **Left out:** Kibo, Canadarm2, the modules added since 2016, the pane's bump shield, the labels' text, and the streaks on the glass that the mock-ups had by day (they didn't read).
  - **The inside is brighter than a camera shows it:** exposed for the Earth, the real one is black but for the copper line. And its night is the day's painting dimmed, since no photograph was found of the dome in the dark.
  - **The station's own light:** real arrays and ships turn through the light; ours are lit from one side for good.

### Its settings
`PixelOrbit.knobs`, listed among the airliner's in `PixelWindowSeat.knobs`.
- **Earth orbit** (`seat.orbit`).
- **Seat** (`seat.array`): By the solar array (the default) or Clear of it. From the Cupola it shows or hides the station's hardware outside the dome instead.
- **Window** (`seat.window`): the airliner's, with its sizes and None. It does nothing from the Cupola, where the dome is the frame and fills the canvas.
- **Looking** (`seat.orbitLooking`): Out the side (the default), Ahead, Straight down, Into the Cupola or From the Cupola (stored 0 to 4).
- **Speed** (`seat.speed`): 1× is the station's real pace; the slider's 0.5× to 10× give an orbit in 186 minutes to 9. Held at 1× while Follow the real ISS is on.
- **Follow the real ISS** (`seat.follow`, off by default), right under Speed, which it holds: the window is over the ground the real station is crossing. One request a minute to wheretheiss.at, shared with Earth from Orbit and Live Sky.
- **Clouds** (`seat.clouds`) and **Lightning in far-off storms** (`seat.lightning`): the airliner's.
- **Today's real clouds** (`seat.realClouds`, off by default), under Clouds: the live cloud map, fetched as Earth from Orbit fetches it (only when its cached copy is over 3 hours old).

### Tuning constants
- **Orbit:** `Orbit.height` 420 km, `tilt` 51.63°, `pace` 5,579.3° a day, `nodePace` −4.95° a day.
- **View:** tipped 25° (out the side and ahead); `lens.focal` 1.2 pane heights.
- **Haze:** `1 − e^(−0.085 / slant)`, where `slant` is the cosine of the angle between our line of sight and straight down at the ground, never under 0.06; in 12 steps.
- **Light:** `OrbitPaint.shine` and `veil` (what each of the eight tones does to a colour, and the haze's colour there) and `heights`.
- **Air:** `airTop` 126 km in 14 bands; the three ramps in `OrbitPaint.init`.
- **Weather:** the cover's middle 0.54 and gain 1.3; cloud's three tones at 0.5, 0.63 and 0.8; a shadow's deck 6 km up.
- **Wing:** root at (−8.5, −6.5, 14) m and tip at (−2.2, 0.3, 48) m in the camera's right, up and ahead; half-width 5 m.
- **Cupola** (`PixelCupola`): the eye 2.47 m back (into) or 0.45 m (from within); the windows `round`, `short`, `long`, `tall` and `corner`; a pane's `edge`, `edgeZ` and `lean`; `recess` 0.11 m and `roundRecess` 0.10; `night` (0.30, 0.35, 0.46) and `glint` #e9b06c, which a bezel takes where its direction from its window's centre is more than 117° from the Sun's (a cosine under −0.45). The hardware's places and the shutter's 12° are in `layHardware`, and every colour of the inside is in `paint`.

### Gotchas and shortcuts
- **A cell keeps the Earth as it was when it was laid** (a `ponytail:` in `advance`): the Earth turns under the orbit at up to 0.46 km/s, 43 km in the 94 s the near ground takes to cross, and cells already in view don't follow. The ground therefore slides exactly along the track, 3° off its true heading, and the map is drawn with a slight shear. New cells always use the Earth as it is, so the track itself is right.
- **The wing is not the station's** (a `ponytail:` on `layWing`). A real wing stands across the truss and the truss's joint carries it round once an orbit, 4° a minute. Ours lies along our line of sight where the airliner's wing is and never moves. It was built to roll about its length, and rolled it stood up across the view as a slab, at night a third of the window wide.
- **The orbit is a circle with two rates, not SGP4** (a `ponytail:` on `Orbit`): within 0.04° of the real station ten minutes after its fix, and not checked over longer. The real one is boosted now and then besides, so over weeks ours is simply an orbit like the station's. Follow the real ISS re-fixes it each minute.
- **The Sun out of the side is squeezed into the window** (see The picture). Its glint and the light on the ground use its true place.
- **Lamps are far denser than a video camera sees** from orbit (under 1% of a frame, a few threads on black): this is the long exposures' look. Without it the night half of every orbit is a black pane.
- **Straight down is the map's own pixels:** a cell of the map is 4.9 km and a pixel 1.6, so coasts come out in blocks of three. The 21600-wide map is 1.9 km a cell and looked crisp in a mock-up, but is 230 MB unpacked.
- **Looking ahead resamples as it goes:** ground grows as it nears, so its cells change size on the way, which the other two views never do. Looked at only as stills so far.
- **Today's real clouds arrive late:** a scene built before the live map has loaded lays made-up weather, and real cloud replaces it only as new cells slide in.
- **The orbit stops while the wallpaper is hidden,** as the airliner's flight does (a frame is never counted as more than a tenth of a second), so at 1× it falls behind the real station by however long it was covered. Follow the real ISS puts it back.
- **The Cupola is not the station's either** (the `ponytail:`s on `PixelCupola` and `layHardware`): see Not true to it, under The Cupola.
- **The dome's small details are a pixel on any canvas:** bolts, tags, labels, the copper line and the bezel don't shrink with the dome, so on a portrait display, where it is half the size, they crowd it. The dome's painting alone was looked at on that canvas (135 × 240), where it still reads, and on a tiny one (20 × 15), where it is a smudge of copper and blue.
- **The glint and the warm hardware go by the station's light, not the ground's.** At dawn on the ground the Sun is already 20° above the station's own horizon, so the dome has neither. The mock-up of that moment had both, having no way to know.
- **A cylinder outside is the outline round its two ends,** which is right through the 45° lens and a straight-sided guess through the fisheye, where a long one should bow.
- **Window still makes a new scene from the Cupola,** though it changes nothing there: the dissolve is between two pictures alike.
- **The snapshot's top row of art pixels is half cut off:** 246 rows of 8 device pixels are 1,968, and the picture is 1,964 tall, standing on its foot. To read a snapshot back as art pixels, count rows from the bottom. (The mock-ups' ground was read from the top, and sits a row out against their dome.)
- **`looks`, not `view`:** `SKScene` has a `view`.
- **A missing map is all sea,** not a crash.
- **The Black Marble's unlit land is not black:** it is a dim blue, brightest on sand and ice, and a brightness threshold lit the whole Sahara with lamps. A lamp is warm, or white (`lamps` in earth.py).
- **`hash`, `pack`, `blend`, `scatter` and `rgb` are repeated** from PixelWindowSeat.swift, where they're private, for the reason given under the airliner's gotchas.

### Checking the orbit
- **A place and a moment:** `ORBIT_OVER=22,-80,up ORBIT_DATE=2026-10-10T17:00:00Z SNAPSHOT_DEFAULTS="seat.orbit=1" SNAPSHOT_SCENE="Pixel Window Seat" swift test -c release -Xswiftc -enable-testing --filter everySceneRenders`. `ORBIT_OVER` is a latitude, a longitude, `up` or `down` for the way it's heading, and optionally a fourth word, which starts an orbit later so the wing's gold face is up. `seat.orbitLooking` is 0 out the side, 1 ahead, 2 straight down; `seat.array=1` is the seat clear of the array.
- **The Cupola's six shots,** each `SNAPSHOT_SECONDS=5` with `seat.orbitLooking=3` (into) or `4` (from within), at the mock-ups' moments: by day `ORBIT_OVER=22,-80,up ORBIT_DATE=2026-10-10T17:00:00Z` (both); dawn on the ground `25,75,down` at `00:40:00Z` (into); sunrise at the station `33,9,down` at `03:52:00Z` (from within); by night `30,-82,up` at `04:00:00Z` (both). `seat.array=1` hides the hardware.
- **Against the mock-ups** (2026-10-10, a script outside the repo, with the mock-ups' own `mock.py` for their side): the six shots were read back one pixel to an art pixel. What is the dome's was read off the renders alone (a pixel whose colour by night is its colour by day times the night tint), and what is hardware likewise (its night colour the silhouette of its day colour). In all six the sky is the mock-up's to the pixel (0 of 92,988 differ) and so is the hardware's place (927 pixels into the dome, 1,860 from within). By day and by night every pixel of the inside is the mock-up's colour exactly (77,456 and 71,220). At dawn 0.68% of the inside differs (524 pixels) and at sunrise 1.12% (799), every one of them bezel: the mock-ups glinted at full strength away from a Sun guessed from the picture, and at dawn the station's Sun is too high for any glint (see Gotchas). The hardware's colours are the mock-up's by day and by night, and differ at dawn and sunrise as the glint does. The ground inside the panes differs throughout: the mock-ups' was rendered before the far cloud was smoothed.

## Research
A research agent measured real window-seat footage and collected reference frames on 2026-10-09. Its frames and written sources are listed under Sources. What it found, and what was done with it:
- **Motion** (measured on the Lauertal clip, six features tracked for 40 s): the view slides sideways, parallel to the horizon (0.5 px/s of vertical drift against up to 9.4 sideways); speed grows in a straight line with distance below the horizon and is zero at it; ground at the bottom of the frame takes about 130 s to cross. Angular speed is the aircraft's speed over the distance to the thing. That is this scene's geometry exactly; ours crosses in about 100 s at 1×.
- **Cloud against ground:** low cumulus slid over the ground under it at 1.29 to 1.45 times its speed (four clouds). Ours is 1.28.
- **The horizon** sat 36 to 52% from the top with the camera level; ours is at 38%.
- **Everything is blue on blue,** and the first rows under the horizon are haze with no detail. Taken as the veil and the haze curve, though ours stays more colourful than a photograph: the agent's own shrink test found a real city from cruise, at 150 rows, is only speckle with a river line, and advised drawing features about three times true size, which is the liberty Dave had already picked.
- **Ocean:** flat deep blue, paler to the horizon; a ship is a pale dash with a wake four to five times its length; surf a broken white line on a thin beach; shallows a lighter rim. All taken.
- **City by day:** a finer, lighter texture than the country round it, the river the darkest thing, parks dark gaps, downtown a small knot of taller blocks, highways thin pale curves, warehouse roofs the brightest specks. Taken, except rail yards and quarries.
- **City by night:** main roads as continuous orange lines in older cities, newer ones mostly white, dark gaps for parks and water, a crisp lit shoreline. Taken as the avenues, the two kinds of district and the dark sea. Only 1.5 to 3% of pixels were lit in its stills (all from climb or approach); ours are denser (see Gotchas).
- **Countryside:** the American square-mile grid with roads on the lines and irrigation circles 800 m across inside the squares; green crops, gold wheat, brown fallow. Taken. Europe's irregular fields were not.
- **Clouds:** cumulus as small bright lumps with a darker shadow on the ground offset away from the Sun, flattening with distance into a white line under the horizon, in clumps and lines rather than evenly; a deck with ragged holes; towers and anvils as the one thing that pokes above the horizon. Taken. Its advice on blending was the plan already in hand: define cover in ground coordinates and let each row slide at its own speed, so a deck arrives as a line under the horizon and no crossfade is needed.
- **Sunset:** the cloud floor goes dark before the sky; tops warm and hollows blue-grey; the ground in blue shadow while cumulus tops are still cream; the wing a silhouette; the wall and reveal glowing orange. The Sun reaches the aircraft until it's about 3.4° below the ground's horizon. Taken as the later Sun for cloud and wing and the reveal's light.
- **The aircraft's lights:** red on the left wingtip, green on the right, white strobes at the tips flashing 40 to 100 times a minute, once on a Boeing and twice on an Airbus. Ours flashes once.
- **The window:** a rounded rectangle with big corners (radius 20 to 30% of its width), a dark seal, a stepped reveal 8 to 18% of the glass's width, near-black wall and a mid-grey reveal when exposed for outside, with a bright wedge where the Sun falls on it; the shade's lip with a centred tab about 28% of the glass's width; a pinhead breather hole near the bottom. Taken. Frost in real photographs is feathery needles or ferny stars across the pane, not a ring round the hole; ours is a few specks.
- **Lightning** (timed on one clip of a very active storm, frame by frame): the cloud lights from inside as a soft blob 5 to 10% of the frame's width; 19 bursts in 40 s; a burst is one to seven flickers over 0.03 to 1.8 s, mostly under a second; gaps of 0.7 to 3 s. Taken for the look and the shape of a burst, at about a tenth of that storm's rate: ours beat every 6 to 13 s and miss one beat in four.
- **Mountains, desert and snow:** mountains with snow patches and blue-shadowed valleys, cumulus sitting on the peaks; orchards on desert as solid dark green rectangles on beige, "already pixel art"; snow turning the field pattern to high-contrast white and brown; snow and fjords, white with blue shadow and near-black water. All taken.
- **Not found:** footage from inside cloud, of moonlit cloud tops, or of night from cruise height; and no pixel-art window-seat piece it could verify, so what reads at this size comes from its own shrink test: the horizon and its haze band, white clouds with offset shadows, one dark river or coast line, big field blocks and a fixed wing edge survive; streets, buildings and wave texture do not.
- **Blocked,** and not worked round: Wikimedia's media host rate-limited it twice (three clips never came down), a search engine served a bot check, and three pages returned 403 (marked below).

### In orbit
A second research agent went through NASA's photographs and raw video from the station on 2026-10-10: 52 stills looked at, six real-time clips (three measured for motion, two timed through a sunrise or sunset, one for night) and 16 frames shrunk to the pane's size. What it found, and what was done with it:
- **The geometry is plain and can be computed.** It measured how the ground moves in a raw downlink (patches matched between frames 2 or 4 s apart) and fitted a ball turning once an orbit under a fixed camera: 21 vectors to 0.5 px/s, about 5%. Its table for this pane matches what was built: the limb 38% down with the view tipped 25.8°, the bottom row crossing in 94 s, 2.1 to 7.6 km a pixel, the nearest ground 573 km away. The limb's curve, fitted on five photographs of known lens, is a circle of radius 2.73 focal lengths.
- **The limb moves, and the near edge is only four times as fast as the far one,** unlike the airliner's horizon. Looking aft everything streams toward one point on the limb, speed in proportion to distance below it. Taken as the strips along parallels.
- **Cloud is flat from orbit.** A top 10 km up moves 1.03 to 1.06 times as fast as the ground behind it (the airliner's low cumulus: 1.29 to 1.45) and stands 1.3 px near the limb and 3.3 at the pane's foot; no sliding was seen in any clip. So cloud is painted on the ground and its shadow carries the relief.
- **The air by day** goes from black to full brightness in 27 to 33 km, 3 to 4 px here: #0c1420, #1b3b60, #416a97, #95bbdb, then pale haze over the first rows of Earth. Shrunk, only the brightest one or two read, so ours is drawn a little taller, to 58 km.
- **At sunrise and sunset** (heights above where it goes black): navy from 70 km, blue near 60, pale at 52, white from 51 to 39, cream at 35, orange at 31, brown-red at 23, nearly black at 14. Taken as the dusk ramp.
- **The airglow** is a thin green line 96 to 97 km above the limb (two photographs), 12 px up this pane, with a yellow-orange layer under it fading to brown over about 30 km, then a darker gap. Taken as the night ramp. In the shrink test its green vanished unless given its own colours.
- **A sunrise in real time** (an external camera's raw downlink): a faint dark-blue arc for about 50 s, a red-orange fleck on its inner edge, 30 s from that to a white Sun, and the ground still black minutes later. Computed: the Sun's disc takes 8 s to cross the limb, and the station is lit 311 s before the ground under it and after. On the ground (a 56-minute clip looking down): first light to day in 100 s, most of it in 40, blue first; cloud tops stay visible 20 to 40 s after the sea has gone dark; 45 minutes of daylight between.
- **Stars** show only in long exposures at night: none in any day or twilight frame, or in any real-time video. Ours are dimmed in sunlight and kept at night.
- **The arrays:** the back of a wing is amber film that glows with the Sun behind it (#d9ad36, #e8ab42, highlights #ffe564) and is salmon-brown in plain daylight (#7f5b47 to #997661); the cell face is near black with a pale grid (#29232c, #dad1dc); by night brown (#381a0d) or copper. A wing is 35 × 12 m, two blankets and a mast in a gap about a fifth of its width, with a fold every 0.43 m: draw about one grid line in three. Ours keeps the mock-up's gold by day, which Dave picked, where a real one would be brown.
- **Window frames:** the Cupola's are matte black with stepped ridges, one thin bright line where the bezel catches the light, small silver bolts and a bar 15 to 20% of a pane's width between panes; a Dragon's is a thick ring of dark bands. Exposed for a sunlit Earth the cabin is black. Taken as the frame.
- **By day everything is blue-grey** and low in contrast but cloud, glint and shadow: deep ocean #142a3f to #223b51, land seen slantwise nearly the sea's colour, cloud grey-white rather than white, and the darkest thing a tall cloud's shadow. Ours is more colourful, as the airliner's is.
- **By night, stills and video disagree:** nearly every NASA night photograph is a long exposure (city cores #fdcda5, 16 to 27% of a city's region lit), and real-time video of Houston is a web of thin yellow-olive lines on black with under 1% of the frame lit. Ours follows the stills.
- **What survives at the pane's size** (the shrink test): the limb's curve and the sunrise's bands, a back-lit gold array 100 to 200 px long (the strongest thing in any frame), the arm and a docked ship as shapes, a window's outline, a tall cloud with its shadow, a cyclone, the Sun's glint as one cream blob, big cities as blobs, a lit coast. What doesn't: the daytime air, twilight ground without dither, every small saturated accent, stars, far lightning, small towns, an array's grid under 15 px wide.
- **The light through an orbit** (computed for 92.9 minutes with the Sun in the orbit's plane): 46 minutes over daylit ground, 5 lit over ground already dark, 36 in shadow, 5 lit before the ground's dawn. With the Sun 60° out of the plane the night is 24 minutes, and above about 70° there is none.
- **Not found:** a real-time clip of lightning from orbit, or of a night pass with the limb in frame, or of the station itself passing into shadow; gas flares; forest, deltas and sea ice by day in a wide view; a view through a Zvezda or Kibo window with hardware in it.
- **Blocked,** and not worked round: NASA's old map pages redirect to a front page (the files still answer), and its Gateway has no record for nine of the hardware frames, so no lens or Sun angle for those. Wikimedia Commons, NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio, ESA, JAXA and Roscosmos were not consulted, for time.

### The Cupola
A third research agent went through NASA's photographs of the Cupola and the papers on its windows on 2026-10-10. It opened every frame listed under Sources (some only at contact-sheet size, named below), fitted a model of the dome to one of them, measured colours in boxes checked by eye, and shrank four frames to the canvas's size. Sourced numbers and its own measurements are kept apart here as it kept them. What it found, and what was done with it:
- **What is sourced:** the windows' clear openings. The round one is 70.6 cm across (the often-quoted 80 cm is the glass) in an assembly 20.8 cm thick. A side one is a trapezium with a short side of 40.0 cm, a long side of 64.4 cm, a height of 40.5 cm and corners of 3.24 cm radius, in an assembly 18.5 cm thick. Both are from slide 12 of ESA's Deloo slides, and NASA JSC's sheet repeats them. Overall the Cupola is 1.5 m high and 2.955 m across with its shutters closed (eoPortal), on a base of 2 m (the JSC sheet). The bar between two side panes (a mullion, ESA's word) carries two seat tracks, and a continuous "upper handrail" runs under the side windows.
- **What is measured:** everything else about the shape, by fitting a model to S130-E-009694 (a Nikon D2Xs at 19 mm by NASA's record, 64 × 45°, which is the pane's own lens), with 12 free numbers. 96% of the model's outline lands within 12 px of the photograph's edge of sky; the error is 2.6 px RMS and 1.3 px at the median at 1280 wide, 0.8 and 0.4 art pixels. The eye comes out 2.47 m from the round window and 5 cm off the dome's axis, up in Tranquility. Checked on a second frame, ISS022-E-066963, with the shape frozen and only the camera free: 80% within 12 px, 4.0 px RMS, 2.4 at the median, the misses lying behind a silhouette in the foreground and the workstation. **The panes' lean, the bars' width and the depth of the recess are fitted, and in no source.**
- **The model,** from the round window's centre, with z out toward the Earth, x forward and y to starboard:
  - The round window: radius 0.353 m at z = 0 (sourced).
  - A side pane facing forward (measured, but for its outline): its short edge's midpoint 0.505 m from the axis at z = −0.016, its long edge's 0.697 m from the axis at z = −0.373, so its corners are (0.505, ±0.200, −0.016) and (0.697, ±0.322, −0.373). The others are that one turned about z. The pane leans 28.4° from the dome's axis (its outward normal is 61.6° from z).
  - The bar between two side panes, glass to glass (measured): 0.18 m at the short edges and 0.16 m at the long. From the round window to a short edge: 0.15 m.
  - The recess (measured): the edge of the sky behaves as a second copy of each outline 0.11 m further out along the pane's normal, 0.10 m for the round one. The ridged wall between the two shows on the side of each pane nearest the eye's axis.

## Ideas / next steps
- **A train:** a third window, on a slow train at ground level, picked by Dave on 2026-10-10. Researched and mocked up, not built: [its plan](pixel-window-seat/train.md).
- **More kinds of country:** islands and reefs, Europe's irregular fields, river mouths with lagoons, lakes in the mountains, a glacier down a cold valley, volcanoes, the seasons (snow over the temperate fields in the user's own winter).
- **Mountains taller than the cloud's base,** drawn properly: paint ground and cloud together in order of distance, with the depth of what is already there kept per pixel, so a high crest hides the cloud beyond it.
- **More sky:** thin cirrus above us as bands near the horizon; a second, higher deck, which by the same law would slide 2.2 times as fast as the ground.
- **Storms:** a thin channel of lightning now and then, a glow on the ground and cloud round a strike, storms that tower nearer than the far rows (they need their own lumpy drawing: see Gotchas), a slider for how often.
- **Passing through cloud:** grey-white with the wingtip fading out. No footage was found for it.
- **Real weather** as a switch: cloud cover from `LiveWeather` where you are, at no extra requests.
- **The window:** real frost patterns that grow at cruise and at night; fine scratches that light up toward the Sun; the cabin's reflection in the glass at night; a 787's shadeless, taller pane as a choice; two real-shaped windows side by side (the choice Dave didn't take).
- **The wing:** a seat behind it, with flap fairings; an engine for the seat ahead; a second strobe flash for an Airbus.
- **City:** rail yards, an airport's runways, a stadium as a white blaze at night, cars' headlights along the highways, tower shadows; a desert city in the colours of its ground.
- **The sea at night:** fishing fleets as clusters of white lights.
- **One display continuing the next:** two displays could be two windows a seat row apart, each a little further along the track.

**For Earth orbit:**
- **Ask Dave about the wing:** flat and changing face at midnight, or the real thing, a wing across the truss turning like a clock hand once an orbit.
- **A finer map straight down:** the 21600-wide Blue Marble, kept in tiles near the track, or the map's edges crinkled with noise.
- **Where we are:** a line of text ("Over the Gulf of Mexico"), most of all with Follow the real ISS on.
- **Cloud worth looking at:** cyclones with an eye, streets, open cells, anvils with shadows two or three times their length near the terminator, a second cloud layer only if it ever reads.
- **The night:** aurora along the limb at high latitudes, fishing fleets' blue-white swarms, lightning as a blob 5 to 10 px across, the Moon's glint.
- **More station:** the robot arm, a docked Soyuz or Dragon, a roll-out array over the old one, the frame catching the Earth's light.
- **More Cupola** (it is two choices of Looking now, not the Window choice first thought of): a seat at one side pane looking to the horizon, the third view, which was not mocked up or built; Canadarm2, once a frame fixes which pane it shows in; the dome's true bearing, a bar dead ahead, as an option.
- **The seasons:** the Blue Marble has a map for each month.
- **A setting for where in the orbit to look,** as the airliner previews a time of day.
- **The website:** a card and a loop of its own. The README has two loops (2026-10-10), made outside `site/media.sh`.

## Checking it
- `SNAPSHOT_SCENE="Pixel Window Seat" swift test` renders now, wherever the flight is.
- **A particular place, time and setting:** `SEAT_AT=30500 SNAPSHOT_DEFAULTS="seat.previewTime=1,seat.previewHour=10.5" SNAPSHOT_SCENE="Pixel Window Seat" swift test -c release -Xswiftc -enable-testing`. `SEAT_AT` is the km along the track the flight starts from. `seat.scenery` is 0 changing, 1 ocean, 2 city, 3 countryside, 4 mountains, 5 desert, 6 snow; `seat.clouds` 0 changing, 1 clear, 2 scattered, 3 broken, 4 overcast; `seat.wing=1` is the seat ahead of the wing; `seat.window` 0 small, 1 medium, 2 large, 3 none; `seat.looking` is the menu's index (7 is west); `seat.lightning=0` turns the lightning off.
- **Settings:** `SETTINGS_SHOT="Pixel Window Seat" swift test --filter settingsWindow` shows its page with the scene behind the header.
- **Tests** (`WindowSeatTests.swift`): `windowSeatCyclesScenery` flies 40,000 km and expects a fair share of the track for each kind of country (at least 15% each for ocean and city, 10% countryside, 8% mountains, 4% each desert and snow) and nine stretches in ten of each to be under 400 km. `windowSeatFliesAtAnySize` builds the scene for a laptop, a portrait display, an ultrawide one and two tiny ones, with each size of window and with none, and expects each to fly on. `windowSeatLightningIsRare` expects every storm rhythm to be lit for more than 20 and fewer than 900 of five minutes' 9,000 thirtieths of a second.
