# Pixel City

A pixel-art city that follows the real Sun and clock. The sky moves through dawn, day, dusk and night, with the Sun and Moon in their real places and stars after dark. Windows light up and go dark through the evening, cars and buses run in both lanes (with headlights at night), a beacon blinks on the tallest tower, and the odd plane crosses. Settings picks the city:

- **Waterfront** (the default, from 2026-10-04): a downtown seen across water, which mirrors it in ripples. The skyline peaks in the middle and falls away to low flanks, so the Sun and Moon rise and set in view, and the walls are lit from wherever the Sun really is.
- **Foothills** (2026-10-04): a small downtown far off under three ridges of mountains, the highest snow-capped, across a valley floor with a highway and a line of pines.
- **Long Bridge** (2026-10-04): a suspension bridge across a bay, strung with lights at night, with a small city and a headland on the far shore and a freighter that crosses now and then.
- **Hillside Town** (2026-10-04): tiled houses terraced up a hill above a harbour, a church at the top, boats at their moorings, and a lighthouse at the end of the breakwater with the open sea beyond.
- **Overlook** (2026-10-04): the view from a rooftop over a sea of other roofs, with their tanks, stair heads and gardens, to a skyline on the horizon.
- **Airport** (2026-10-04): an airfield across a bay, with airliners landing, taxiing to a stand in front of the terminal, waiting, and taxiing out to take off again, into the real wind.
- **Spaceport** (2026-10-05): a launch site across a lagoon, seen from the bank the public watches from. Fourteen rockets, from Mercury-Redstone to Starship, roll out and lift off on a column of smoke; the Falcons' boosters come back and land, and a crane carries them away; Starship's is caught by its tower; and the Shuttle's orbiter glides home to a runway.

- **Files:** `Sources/Atrium/PixelCity.swift`, which holds everything: the scene, every city, the `Pixels` canvas, sprite art as strings, `SeededRandom`, the water's shader and its own low-precision `skyPosition()` for the Sun. It reads `Location.swift` ([live-sky.md](live-sky.md)) and, for the Airport's wind, `LiveWeather.swift` ([weather.md](weather.md)).
- **Entry:** `pixelCity(size:)` builds `final class PixelCity: SKScene`. Its entry in Scenes.swift is "Pixel City", icon `building.2.fill`, tint `.pink`, with `PixelCity.knobs`.
- **Kind:** SpriteKit on a low-resolution canvas. Two backdrop textures are repainted every 30 s, the sky and the city in front of it, and sprites for the moving things all sit under a `canvas` node scaled up by the pixel size. Every texture uses `.nearest` filtering, so it stays crisp.

## How it works
- **Resolution:** `pixel = max(2, round(height/240))` points per art pixel, so 4 pt on a 982 pt-tall display. The canvas is `w × h` art pixels (378 × 246 there). Motion rounds to whole art pixels so nothing blurs.
- **Layers,** by `zPosition`: the sky (0), clouds (1), the plane (1.5), the city (2), the water (2.5), cars (3 and 4), the Airport's aircraft (4.2 to 4.6), the Spaceport's layer of moving things (4.5), the beacon (5; 4.4 at the Spaceport, so its smoke hides the beacon as it hides the tower). The city texture is clear wherever the sky shows, so clouds and planes pass behind the towers.
- **A city is a case of `City`** (the menu's order, stored as the index, so new ones go at the end, and a city cut from anywhere but the end needs a new key: see Settings). `layOut()` sets what differs: `ground` (the row the city stands on), `waterRows`, `crest` (for each column, the row where the sky begins, which is the horizon the Sun and Moon rise over) and `skyBase` (the lowest of those, where the sky's gradient starts).
- **`layOut()`** builds everything that never changes, and runs again when Settings picks another city. Each city has its own skyline (`layOutWaterfront`, `layOutFoothills`, `layOutBridge`, `layOutHillside`, `layOutOverlook`, `layOutAirport`, `layOutSpaceport`) from a fixed `SeededRandom`, so it's identical on every redraw and every display. Shared by all: 170 stars, 3 cloud masks, a pool of 6 cars per lane with headlights (7 sedan colours plus a bus), a plane with blinking nav lights, and the beacon (0.25 s on, 1.25 s off) on the landmark.
- **`redraw()`** runs at init, every 30 s and when a setting changes. It paints the sky into one `Pixels` buffer and the city into another:
  - **Sky:** a gradient between zenith and horizon colours from `skyColours(elevation, morning:)`. The stops run −18°, −10°, −4°, 0°, 6° and 15°, and dawn is pinker than dusk. Around a low Sun the horizon's colour changes with the compass (`horizonToward`): orange under the Sun and, opposite it, the pink band over the Earth's shadow, so a sunset sky differs from one side of the screen to the other. It's quantised into 14 bands with 4×4 Bayer dithering, plus a sun glow around a low Sun in 5 flat rings, dithered only where two rings meet (dithering right across each ring left a halo of loose dots).
  - **Stars** fade in below about −5°; the brightest get a small cross.
  - **Moon:**
    - **Phase:** from a mean synodic month counted from the new Moon of 2000-01-06 18:14 UTC.
    - **Position:** the Sun's ecliptic longitude plus phase × 360° (no lunar inclination).
    - **Lit side:** on the right while waxing.
    - **Earthshine** on the dark part at night.
    - **Size:** an 8-pixel radius. A thin crescent is drawn at least 1.6 pixels wide, because at this size a true one breaks into specks.
  - **Sun:** a 6-pixel-radius disc, orange near the horizon.
  - **The Waterfront's street** (`drawStreet`, standing on `streetBase`): sidewalks, road markings, and a lamp every 46 pixels casting a three-step pool of light at night.
  - Clouds, cars and the plane are recoloured for the light: day and night car textures, the plane turning into a dark silhouette, and headlights fading in at night. A headlight is a pool of light lying on the road ahead, added to the road's colour (`blendMode = .add`) and drawn under the car in front. Clouds are solid, so they hide the stars behind them.
- **`update(_:)`** runs every frame:
  - **Cars:** they move at their cruise speed (buses 11–14, cars 14–22 art pixels per second). A car closes up behind a slower car in the same lane (gap < 6) instead of driving through it.
  - **Spawning:** new cars come in from the pool every `(1.5–6 s) / traffic`, where `traffic` is an hourly table with rush hours at 08:00 and 17:00 and quiet small hours. 10% of spawns are buses.
  - **Clouds** drift and wrap.
  - **Plane:** one every 40–120 s, at 7 px/s, somewhere in the top 20% of the sky.

### The Waterfront city
Built from a design review on 2026-10-04 (see Dave's feedback below), after a look at a few openly licensed pixel-art cityscapes on OpenGameArt for what makes them read: side walls in a darker tone, lights in sparse dashes rather than a grid, stepped silhouettes, several depth rows. Nothing was copied; all of it is drawn in code.

- **Composition,** bottom to top: water (the bottom 21%, calm, and where the Dock sits), a 6-row quay wall, the street on top of it (`streetBase` is above the water here, so the traffic clears the Dock), then the city, with a far shore of low hills (`drawHorizon`) behind the gaps.
- **Skyline** (`layOutWaterfront`, seed 2030, picked from six for its shape and for parks where the Sun sets): `downtown(x)` is 1 across the middle quarter and falls to 0 by 30% either side of centre, and every height is a share of the sky above the street scaled by it. Three rows:
  - **Haze:** windowless towers behind downtown only (`Kind.haze`), 80% of the way to the sky's colour, with a few specks of light at night.
  - **Far:** stone, glass and slab buildings at half the detail. On the flanks half the plots are left open.
  - **Near:** downtown is stone and glass towers and slabs; the flanks are brick walk-ups, slabs and parks (40% of flank plots, `drawParks`: a hedge and round trees lit from the Sun's side). Buildings touch or stand 3 or more pixels apart, because a 1-pixel gap glows like a glitch at dusk.
  - **The landmark:** the widest near building around 55% across becomes a stone tower two thirds of the sky tall, with a crown, a spire and the beacon.
- **Four kinds of building** (`Kind`, drawn by `drawTower`):
  - **`deco`:** stone tiers stepping in to a crown, windows in tall strips between piers, a lit doorway.
  - **`glass`:** a curtain wall that mirrors the sky (zenith at its top, horizon at its foot, so it warms at sunset), a dark line at every floor, mullions, a lobby, and a flat, slanted or plant-room top.
  - **`brick`:** a walk-up with sash windows and sills under a cornice, a fire escape on about half, a shop under a striped awning (lit from 7 until it closes, between 8 and 11 at night), and a tank, stair head or chimney on the roof.
  - **`slab`:** concrete with ribbon windows, on columns over an open ground floor.
- **Light with a direction.** `ambient` is what the sky gives every wall (cooler toward dusk, moonlit blue at night). The Sun, and after dark the Moon scaled by its real phase, add to walls that face them: `keyRight` and `keyLeft` for side walls, `keyFront` for fronts, `keyTop` for roof edges, each the cosine of the angle between the light and that wall. By default we look toward the equator, so the Sun is behind the city for most of the day: fronts stay in the sky's cool light and only catch the Sun when it's behind us (summer mornings and evenings), while side walls light up warm at one end of the day and fall into shadow at the other. Looking another way (Settings → Looking) changes all of it: face east and the fronts turn gold at sunset, with the Sun out of the picture behind us. Glass fronts mirror the horizon at our backs (`behind`). `lit(colour, key, shade:)` applies it.
- **Side walls.** Each building shows a sliver of the wall facing the middle of the screen (`side`, 1 to 5 pixels, wider toward the edges), as one-point perspective would. So in the morning the walls on show right of centre are sunlit, and in the evening those left of centre.
- **Windows in dashes** (`storey`): a floor's windows are lit a run of neighbours at a time. Homes (brick, and half the slabs) keep evening hours (`isLit`, with three seeded draws per run: people get home between 16:30 and 21:00 and go to bed between 21:00 and 03:00; some rise early, from 05:30; 3% never switch off), with 45% of rooms on that schedule, in warm light of varying brightness. Offices (the rest) light `officeShare` of their runs: half at dusk, falling to 8% by midnight and rising again from 05:30, in cool white on most glass and slab buildings and warm on stone. Each run's draw is fixed by the building's seed, so as the share falls the same runs go dark in the same order. Taller roofs carry red corner lights.
- **Where the Sun and Moon land** (`place`): azimuth spans about 260° across the screen, so sunrise and sunset stay on screen all year at mid latitudes. Elevation runs as the 0.75 power of elevation / 70°, which gives a low Sun more room, and a body below the horizon drops out of sight within about half a degree. The horizon it sets behind is `crest` at that column, which it clears by 15° up.
- **Sky:** the same gradient, with only 40% of each band dithered into the next (`band`), the city's own mauve glow at night, strongest low over downtown, and stars that fade out toward the horizon.
- **Water** (`addWater`, `waterShader`, shared by every city with water): one sprite with a shader that reads the sky and city textures. Each water row shows the quay wall, then the city from street level up, squashed 1.8 times (the street lies flat and out of sight). Bright things (the Sun, the Moon, lamps) keep their brightness in it, and anything the city's painting has standing in the water shows over it. Rows slide sideways by whole pixels on two sine waves, wider toward the viewer, the image fades toward a deep-water colour, and short dashes of sky colour drift on every other row. It animates by `u_now`.
- **Moving things in the water** (`reflect`, `mirrorTraffic`, added 2026-10-05): the shader mirrors the two paintings, and the things that move aren't in them. So each one's own picture goes to the shader as well (`u_thing0`, `u_thing1`…) with where it is (`u_place`: the column its picture starts from, the water row its foot is mirrored at, and its size, the width negative when it faces left). The shader lays its mirror image into the water before the ripples, the squash, the deep-water tint and the glints, so it moves exactly as a building's image does.
  - **Where the image goes:** something floating (a boat, the freighter) is mirrored from its own waterline. Something on land is mirrored from the foot of the wall's image, where the buildings' images start, and lower by its height over 1.8 (the water's squash) as it rises.
  - **Traffic** is too many sprites to hand over one by one, so every car on the road is painted each time one moves into a single strip the width of the screen (`trafficStrip`, a mutable texture: 10 rows on the Waterfront, 2 on the Long Bridge, 1 at the Airport), and the strip is mirrored as one thing. Headlights and tail lights show in the water at night; the pools of light on the road don't, since they lie flat.
  - **What each city mirrors:** the Waterfront its cars and buses; the Long Bridge the traffic on its deck and the freighter; Hillside Town its six boats; the Airport its four aircraft and the apron's vans; the Spaceport the one layer everything of its that moves is painted into.

### The Foothills city
The first of the cities Dave picked from the research below. Everything the Waterfront has (the light, the sky, windows in dashes) applies; what's new is the land.

- **Composition,** bottom to top: a dark bank of pines (under the Dock), a valley floor of fields with a highway across it, the city band on `ground` (27% up), then three ridges and the sky.
- **Mountains** (`layOutFoothills`, `drawMountains`, seed 2041): each ridge is five octaves of noise between random knots, the first two folded (`1 − |2v − 1|`) so the peaks come to points. Far to near they are rock with snow above a wavering line, lower rock with a few snow tips, and forested foothills, each hazier than the one in front.
  - **Faces:** a pixel's face slopes right, left or toward us by the difference in the ridge's height either side of it, over a span that widens downhill (2 pixels at the crest to 16), so light and shadow run down from the peaks in streaks instead of the mountain being one flat tone. One side is always the brighter, the one the Sun is on.
  - **The Sun counts as higher the higher up a face is** (up to 5° at the top), so the peaks glow after the valley is in shade and light first at dawn.
- **`crest`** follows the highest ridge, so the Sun and Moon rise and set behind the mountains themselves. Real mountains seen from a city stand only a few degrees high; these fill a third of the screen, so the true horizon would hide the Sun for hours.
- **The city band:** the Waterfront's far-row buildings (stone, glass, slab, with windowless towers behind) at a third of their usual haze (`farHaze`), a downtown 36% across (`downtown`) and low buildings with open ground elsewhere. The landmark is the tallest, with a mast and the beacon.
- **The valley floor** (`drawPlain`, seed 77): six bands of grass paling toward the city, strips of field, 70 houses and trees thickening toward town (a house lights one window at night on the homes' hours), and a pine bank along the bottom, each tree lit on the Sun's side.
- **The highway:** the same traffic as the other cities at a distance. `speck` draws a car as a dash of paint by day and only its tail light and headlight by night; they run at 0.35 of the usual speed (`carPace`) from a pool of 14 a lane.

### The Long Bridge city
The second of Dave's picks. It reuses the Waterfront's water and the Foothills' far-off city band and traffic; what's new is the bridge.

- **Composition,** bottom to top: the bay (everything below `ground`, the far shore, 32% up), the bridge standing in it in front of the far shore, hills on that shore rising to a headland on the right (`hill`, which also sets `crest`), a small city at their left end (`layOutBand` with no suburbs, `downtown` 13% across), and the sky. The open water and sky are on the right, where desktop icons sit.
- **The bridge** (`drawBridge`, `cable`, `towers`, `deck`, `towerTop`): two towers 22% and 78% across, their footings 12 rows short of the far shore, the roadway 14 rows above it on a truss, and tops 36% of the sky higher. The main cable is a parabola between the tower tops and a nearly straight run from each down to an anchorage off screen, with a hanger every 6 pixels. It's far steeper than a real one (about 1 in 4, where real spans are nearer 1 in 10), because true proportions would leave the towers 20 pixels tall. The steel is a grey-green of our own, so it isn't the Golden Gate.
- **At night:** a lamp where each hanger meets the cable, one every 8 pixels along the deck, the towers floodlit from the roadway up, a red light on each top and the blinking beacon on the left one. All of it is mirrored in the bay.
- **The water** mirrors about two lines: below the towers' footings it reflects everything from there up, and the strip between the footings and the far shore reflects only the foot of that shore (squashed 0.7), or the bridge would be reflected twice.
- **Traffic:** `speck` cars on two rows of the deck.
- **The freighter** (`freighter`, `shipTexture`): one crosses in front of the bridge at 2.2 pixels a second every 1.5 to 4 minutes, mirrored by the water from its own waterline. By night its hull is a shadow with lit bridge windows and a mast light.

### The Hillside Town city
The third of Dave's picks, and the only one with no towers: a Mediterranean town, whose pale walls take the Sun's colour.

- **Composition:** the sea fills everything below its horizon (`ground`, 26% up). The land stands in it on the left: a quay whose waterline is `harbour` (14% up, well below the horizon because it's near us), and above it a hill (`slope`) half the screen tall at the left edge that falls to the water 66% of the way across. A breakwater runs on to 86%, with the lighthouse near its end, and across the water on the right is a low headland (`hill`). The open sea and sky are on the right, where desktop icons sit, and by default the Sun sets there.
- **Houses** (`layOutHillside`, seed 2063, drawn by `drawHouse`): 13 terraces 10 rows apart, the highest laid first so that nearer houses overlap those behind. A terrace runs from the left edge for as far as the hill stands that high. A house is 9 to 15 wide and two or three storeys, in one of seven wall colours, with a door, windows on the homes' hours (`isLit`), shutters on half of them, and a roof that is hipped, gabled toward us or flat. One plot in five is a gap with a cypress (`Kind.cypress`, in the same list so it's drawn in terrace order).
- **The hill** (`drawHill`, before the houses): scrub with a lit edge and outcrops of rock, and the church near the top: a nave, and a bell tower with a pointed cap, a cross and a belfry that glows at night.
- **The harbour** (`drawHarbour`, after them): the quay with a lamp every 18 pixels, the breakwater, the lighthouse (white with red bands; the beacon is its lantern, flashing white here), and two sails hull down on the horizon.
- **Boats:** six sprites at moorings off the quay (`fishingBoat`, `sailboat`, painted by `afloat`, and mirrored by the water from their own waterlines, like the freighter), each rising and falling one pixel in its own time. There is no traffic: `cars` is empty.
- **The water** mirrors the town about the quay's waterline, and the far headland about the horizon. The headland and sails are painted at 0.99 alpha, which the shader reads as "across the water" and leaves out of the near reflection, or they would be mirrored twice.

### The Overlook city
The last of Dave's picks, and the only view from above. The research agent's worry was that the middle distance of roofs would turn to noise at this size and advised testing a still at noon and at night first. It reads: what holds it together is a steady fall in size from band to band, haze that deepens with it, and muted roofs.

- **Composition,** bottom to top: our own roof (`drawRooftop`: a dark parapet along the bottom where the Dock sits, a big water tank at the left, an aerial and two vents), five bands of buildings seen from above, the far skyline standing on `ground` (54% up, from `layOutBand` with a downtown 42% across), low hills behind it, and the sky.
- **Buildings from above** (`layOutOverlook`, seed 2074, `Kind.block`, drawn by `drawBlock`): each is a front wall with its flat roof behind it, two fifths as deep as the building is wide. The bands stand at 5.5%, 20%, 34%, 44% and 50% of the screen's height and shrink from 34–56 pixels wide to 5–9, the far ones laid first so nearer ones overlap them. A building is hazier the farther up it stands, up to half the horizon's colour.
  - **Walls** are in more shade than elsewhere (0.8 of the sky's light), with the usual side wall on show and windows lit in runs: two buildings in three are homes, with 32% of rooms on the evening's hours, the rest offices.
  - **Roofs** take the sky's light plus the Sun from above, with a lit near edge to the parapet. Roofs 5 or more deep carry one thing per 11 pixels of width: a water tank, a stair head, air conditioning, a skylight (some lit at night), a garden, solar panels or nothing, the standing ones with a shadow on the side away from the Sun.
- **Streets** are the gaps between: tarmac by day, and by night a dim warm glow with 420 street lamps scattered over the ground, of which only those no building covers show.
- **Life** (added 2026-10-04, after Dave found the first version had "stray moving white blocks" and not "much life to the city"):
  - **Steam** from five stacks: two on our own roof and one on each of three roofs nearby (`stack(on:)`; those roofs are marked `roof == .antenna`). Each stack has three puffs in turn, sprites that go through five ages (`puffs`): a dab at the vent, then a round cloud that swells, leans a pixel downwind each step, and thins from 55% to 8%. They are tinted by the light they rise through: white by day, warm at dusk, a grey shadow at night. The first version was a 2×2 white square stepping upward, which read as exactly the stray block Dave saw; a puff with holes in it, the second try, read as a symbol. Round and whole is what reads as steam at this size.
  - **Pigeons:** nine birds wheeling over the middle roofs on a wide flat ring that itself drifts, one turn in 26 seconds, each beating its wings in its own time and flashing paler as it banks toward the light. They roost at dusk (hidden once `night` passes 0.35).
  - **Traffic** on an expressway on piers across the foot of the far skyline (`speck` cars, as in Foothills, on rows `ground + 6` and `+ 7`, above every roof so nothing nearer should hide them).
  - **Red lights** on three of the far skyline's taller buildings, each blinking in its own slow time, at night.

### The Airport city
Dave asked for it on 2026-10-04: "a pixel airport would be really cool, with planes taking off and landing, taxing etc". A research agent and the first build arrived separately at the same view, and Dave kept it (see his decisions below).

- **Composition,** bottom to top: a bay (the bottom 21%, mirroring everything, calm under the Dock), a sea wall, then the airfield seen at a low angle, so its strips stack up in a band 25 rows deep: the runway nearest the water, grass, the taxiway, the apron with three stands, and the terminal. Behind are a line of trees, low hills and the far city (`layOutBand`, its downtown 12% across). Hangars and the control tower stand left of the terminal; the right is open grass and sky, where desktop icons sit and, looking the default way, the Sun sets.
- **Why the runway is nearest:** a take-off then leaves the ground in front of everything and climbs straight into the sky, which is 60% of the picture. With the runway behind the apron, flights would be hidden by the terminal.
- **Buildings** (`drawAirfield`): two hangars with curved roofs (one open and lit at night); a terminal 44% of the screen wide, a glass front under a white roof with a taller hall in the middle, lit from inside after dark a few bays at a time (it never closes, but half its lights go off between 23:30 and 05:00); the tower, a concrete shaft 44 rows tall with a cab of dark glass leaning out under its roof; a floodlight mast between each pair of stands, with a pool of light on the apron under it at night; and a windsock on the grass to the right.
- **Aircraft** (`airliner`, `turboprop`, `widebody`, built by `aircraft`): three types drawn as rows of characters, 42, 30 and 60 pixels long, which is about a metre to the pixel. Each is white with its fin and a stripe in one of six colours of our own (no real airline's), and no two on the airfield wear the same one. At most one is a wide-body.
  - **Light:** each is painted in the light it stands in (`dress`): the sky's and the Sun's by day, so white fuselages turn gold and then lilac through a sunset, and at night the apron's floodlights once it's on its stand. Its cabin windows light up after dark. It's repainted when its pitch, wheels or place change and at every 30 s repaint, a 60 × 19 picture at most.
  - **Lights:** a red beacon on its back that flashes while the engines run (from 8 seconds before it leaves its stand), a white strobe at the wingtip on the runway and in the air, a steady wingtip light at night (green when we see its right side, red its left, as on a real aircraft) and a landing light on the approach and the take-off (a bright pixel in the picture, with a small glare over it).
  - **Pitch:** nose-up attitudes are the same art with every column slid up or down whole pixels about the main wheels, which is how pixel art draws a shallow line. The wheels come up once it's 12 pixels off the ground.
- **The round** (`fly`, a `Flight` and its `Phase`): away, down the approach (26 pixels a second, easing off with the nose up over the last 40 pixels), touchdown 30% of the way across, a roll-out slowing to 10 and off the runway's far end; out of sight for 10 to 20 seconds; back along the taxiway at 7 the other way, slowing as it pulls off onto a free stand; a wait there; off the stand and on along the taxiway to the runway's other end; out of sight again; onto the runway, a 4-second pause, then a take-off roll that gathers speed, the nose lifting at 24 pixels a second, and a climb away at up to 36. A real airliner does about 75 metres a second at both ends of a flight; these run at a third to a half of that, so they stay calm next to traffic at 14 to 22.
  - **Nothing turns round in view.** Everything on the runway moves one way and everything on the taxiway the other, and the two ends where an aircraft would turn through 180° are off the screen, so one side-on drawing of each type is all it needs. Stands are taxi-through, parallel to the terminal, as at a small regional airport: no pushback, no jet bridges.
  - **One at a time has the runway**, landings first. On the taxiway an aircraft holds while another is within 80 pixels ahead of it, and one won't leave its stand while another is taxiing past.
  - **Four aircraft share three stands.** A scene starts with two parked (one about to leave), one a few seconds out on the approach and one away, so there's something to see at once.
  - **How busy** (`flying`): a wait on the stand is 2.5 to 5.5 minutes and time away 40 to 150 seconds, both running down at the pace of Settings' Flights by day (1×) or Flights at night (0.7×), easing between the two from 5 to 7 in the morning and from 8 in the evening to midnight. At 1× that's a landing or a take-off about once a minute (18 in a 20-minute test at noon); at night's 0.7×, 3.5 to 8 minutes on the stand. It was first tied to the road traffic's hourly table, which left a parked aircraft waiting 17 to 37 minutes around midnight: see Dave's decisions.
- **Reflections in the bay** (see Moving things in the water, which began here): every aircraft is mirrored. One on the ground is mirrored from the sea wall's image down, like the buildings; as one climbs, its image sinks toward us by its height over 1.8, and slides off the bottom of the screen at about 85 pixels up. Lit windows and the landing light keep their shine in the water, as lamps do.
- **The wind** (`windAlong`, `windWay`): with Settings → Land and take off into the real wind on, the Airport asks `LiveWeather` for the wind where you are (one shared Open-Meteo request, every 15 minutes, only while the Airport is the city showing). The runway lies across the screen, so what counts is the wind's speed along it: aircraft land and take off toward the side it blows from.
  - **A change of direction waits for the airfield to empty.** While the runway in use is wrong for the wind, no more aircraft arrive; those on stands leave the old way; when all four are away the direction changes and they come back the new way. A real airport's traffic pauses for a runway change too. The direction only changes once the wind along the runway reaches 9 km/h against it: the 5 knots at which a real airport goes by the wind (FAA AIM 4-3-6), and enough that a light wind across doesn't flip it back and forth.
  - **The windsock** streams downwind and is fully out at 28 km/h along the runway (a real one's 15 knots), drooping below that. With the setting off, or before the first report, it hangs limp and aircraft go to the right.
- **Night:** white lights along both edges of the runway, blue along the taxiway's (their real colours), lit signs on the grass, the floodlights' pools, the terminal's glass and, on the tower's mast, the beacon of a civil land airport: white and green by turns, 27 flashes a minute (the FAA's range is 24 to 30). The bay mirrors the buildings, their lights and the aircraft, but not the airfield lying flat behind the sea wall.
- **Traffic on the apron:** the cities' usual `speck` cars as vans and tugs on a service road along the terminal, behind the stands: three a lane, far fewer than a road's.

### The Spaceport city
Dave asked for it on 2026-10-05 (his words are under his decisions below), and picked the view, the distance and the climb from six mock-ups painted over real renders of the sky and lagoon.

- **Composition,** bottom to top:
  - **Our bank:** grass in shadow along the bottom 6 to 9 rows, with the countdown board on the left, the Stars and Stripes and a second pole for a visiting flag, thirteen people and a camera on a tripod in silhouette, and a palm at the right edge. The Dock hides the middle of it, so the board and the flags are to the left.
  - **A lagoon,** the bottom 23.5%, which mirrors everything across it.
  - **The far shore:** shell sand, a road, scrub and clumps of low trees, cleared round the buildings. Right of the pad the road is a runway: paler, with lights along it after dark.
  - **The site,** left to right: a tall blank assembly building far off in the haze; the hangar, a long white shed with a door at the pad end, lit inside after dark; Starship's pad, a launch mount on legs beside a dark lattice tower 96 rows tall, the tallest thing here; the pad, a mound 6 rows high with a ramp on the hangar's side, the flame trench's mouth, a launch mount, a lattice tower 58 rows tall with an arm and a mast (the beacon is on top), a lightning mast either side, two floodlights and a water tower; two tanks and the liquid oxygen's sphere; and two landing zones, 77% and 89% of the way across, with a hut and a mast between them.
  - **The sky** is three quarters of the picture, for the climb and the trail.
- **Everything that moves is painted into one layer** (`launchLayer`, a mutable texture as wide as the canvas that covers everything above the water, painted by `paintLaunch` into a `Bytes` canvas): the smoke, the glare round each flame, the strongback, the rocket, the flames, the boosters and the crane. Two reasons: smoke has to pass in front of some things and behind others, and the lagoon's shader can then mirror the lot as one thing (see Moving things in the water).   - **What else differs** (`Kit`): how long and wide the flame is (14 by 2 pixels for the Redstone, 52 by 7 for the Saturn V, 44 by 9 for SLS); how thick the trail is (the Shuttle's and SLS's solid boosters leave one 1.7 times a Falcon 9's); how much steam the lift-off raises (the small three raise a third as much, and it stays close in so it holds together as a cloud); how briskly it climbs (the Saturn V at 0.82 of a Falcon's pace, the Shuttle at 1.15); and whether it sheds cold vapour on the pad (the Titan's fuels weren't cold).
  - **Which rocket** (`nextRocket`): only those switched on in Settings fly. Rockets whose boosters come back (the two Falcons and Starship) and rockets that fly once take turns, so there is a landing after every other lift-off however many are switched on. Within each kind every rocket flies once, in a shuffled order, before any flies again, so with all of them on a Falcon Heavy's double landing, like Starship's catch, comes every sixth launch. Each scene keeps its own order (`hangar`), so two displays each get their turns of both kinds; a new scene takes up where the last one to pick left off, with the same rockets still to fly in a fresh order, so the turns carry across the app's Shuffle and a move between cities. A rocket is picked when its wait in the hangar begins, so the board can name it; if it has been switched off by the time it would roll out, another is picked. Half the time a Falcon's boosters have flown before and are sooty toward the foot.
  - **Turning and shrinking** (`stamp`): each pixel under the rocket looks up the art it falls on, so one drawing serves lying on the transporter, standing up, leaning in the climb and shrinking with height. Mid-raise it's jagged, as any turned pixel art is.
  - **Light** (`rocketPaint`): each paint (white, a tank's orange foam, bare metal, cream) has a lit side, a face and a shaded side, from the same Sun as the buildings; on the pad after dark the floodlights have it; in the air its foot takes the flame's light.
- **The round** (`launch`, `Pad.Phase`), three and a half to four and a half minutes from one lift-off to the next:
  - **In the hangar** for 50 to 110 seconds.
  - **Roll-out:** a Falcon, or one of the small three, lies on its strongback, foot first, and rolls out of the hangar's door at 3.5 pixels a second, along the shore and up the ramp (30 seconds). What is still inside the door isn't painted. The four big ones come out standing, as theirs did, on a crawler that comes in from the left edge (the assembly building's side) at the same speed, past the hangar and up the ramp (54 seconds).
  - **The raise:** 18 seconds from lying to standing, hinged at the launch mount. The strongback is as long as most of the rocket, so a Redstone's is short. A rocket that came out standing only settles for 4 seconds.
  - **The count:** 70 seconds of fuelling, with cold vapour drifting off the rocket. The strongback leans back for the last 20.
  - **Lift-off:** the engines light 2.6 seconds early; the deluge's steam rolls out of both ends of the flame trench and up round the mount for ten seconds; the strongback is thrown back, and lowered once the rocket has gone.
  - **The climb** (`ascent`, `lens`), 21 seconds: see below.
  - **Roll-back:** the empty strongback, or the crawler, goes home at 5 pixels a second, and the wait in the hangar starts again.
- **Starship's round** differs, because it has its own pad (`Kit.caught`, `starX`, `site`):
  - **It rolls in standing** on a crawler from the left edge, as the big NASA rockets do, but stops under its tower, short of the ramp.
  - **The tower's arms lift it** onto the launch mount, 9 rows up, in ten seconds. The arms are a carriage on the tower and a beam out across the rocket, just under the booster's grid fins; they rest low, where a crawler leaves a rocket, and are painted with the things that move.
  - **It lifts off** like the rest, with half as much steam again as a Falcon 9 and a thin trail.
  - **Its booster comes back to the tower** 62 seconds later (`Booster` with `zone` 2): the same fall and landing burn as a Falcon's, with no legs, down to the arms, which hold it over the mount.
  - **The pad waits for it** (`Pad.Phase.recover`). After 14 seconds in the arms it is lowered onto the crawler, ten seconds more, and rides home off the left edge. No crane is needed, and no other rocket rolls out until it has gone.
  - **Then its ship comes back** (`Booster.ship`, `pose(ship:)`), 100 seconds after lift-off, to one of the two landing zones: so a Starship's count holds, like a Falcon's, until one is free. It falls on its belly, nose to the right and its black tiles down, at 22 pixels a second, slower than a booster. At 100 pixels up its engines light and it swings upright over the next 45, then comes down on its flame and stands on its skirt, about 20 seconds after it came into view. It is the ship's half of Starship's drawing, turned by `stamp`. The crane carries it off as it does a Falcon's booster, with its hook two rows higher.
- **The Shuttle's orbiter comes home** (`Orbiter`), 150 seconds after a Shuttle's lift-off. It's the Shuttle's own drawing of the orbiter, laid on its belly with its nose to the left (`orbiterArt`, the same rows turned the other way and stamped a quarter turn over), so no second drawing was needed.
  - **The glide:** in from beyond the right edge at 16 pixels a second, 62 rows up, nose down and sinking at 11, which is steep, as a real one's glide was.
  - **The flare:** below 9 rows it sinks more gently and lifts its nose, and its wheels are down.
  - **The roll-out:** it touches down near the right-hand landing zone and slows at 3 pixels a second each second behind a red and white drag chute, stopping after about 43 pixels.
  - **Then** it stands for 12 seconds and a tug tows it off by the tail, the way it came, at 4 pixels a second. A real one was towed nose first.
  - **The runway is the shore road,** in front of the landing zones, so the orbiter is drawn at full size and passes in front of whatever stands on them. A crane working there at the same moment would overlap it; at the usual pace they miss each other by a minute.
- **The climb.** Height is `0.65 t² (1 + t / 40)`, so it clears the tower in about 9 seconds (a real one takes about 7, from memory). The sky is seen through a wide lens (`lens`): true to size for the first 60 pixels up, then squeezed by a logarithm, with everything drawn smaller by the same amount. So the rocket shrinks to a spark and leans over to the right as it climbs, leaving a trail that tapers and arcs; it's in view for about 18 seconds, where at full size it would be out of the top of the picture in 14. Dave picked this over full size and straight up. It only starts to lean once it's above the tower.
- **Boosters** (`Booster`): 62 seconds after lift-off (a real one takes about 8 minutes) the booster comes back in at the top over a landing zone, falling at 34 pixels a second with its grid fins out; from 170 pixels up its landing burn slows it at whatever rate stops it as it touches; its legs come down in the last 26; dust rolls out along the ground. A Falcon Heavy's two side boosters come back 1.3 seconds apart, one to each zone; its centre core flies on.
- **The crane** (`Crane`): 14 seconds after a landing a crawler crane drives in from the right at 5 pixels a second, lowers its hook to the booster's top, lifts it a little (its legs fold) and carries it off the way it came at 4. With two boosters down it takes the nearer one first and comes back.
- **A count holds at ten seconds** while a booster from the last flight is still standing on a landing zone the next one needs (a Falcon Heavy needs both).
- **Smoke** (`Puff`, `puff()`, `paintSmoke`): four kinds of puff, all the same thing with different numbers: vapour off a fuelled rocket, the deluge's steam, the trail (laid from where the flame ended a moment ago to where it ends now, which is 30 pixels back along the rocket's own axis at full size, and smaller the higher it's laid), and a landing's dust.
  - **It moves in steps of a fifteenth of a second,** as hand-drawn smoke would: each puff slows, swells toward its full size, rises if it's big, and drifts on a wind that blows the way the clouds go and harder at some heights than others, which is what kinks a trail.
  - **Painting:** every puff adds a round hump to a field of density. Where that's thick enough there is cloud, in three flat tones: a rim toward the light where the cloud is much thinner two pixels that way, shade where it's much thinner the other way, the body between. "Much thinner" is by ratio, so the tones follow a thick cloud's lobes; by difference, half of every cloud fell into shade. Thin edges are part clear (40% and 72% solid).
  - **Its light:** the Sun's and the sky's by day. After sunset, what is high enough is still in sunlight: the Earth's shadow climbs 26 rows for each degree the Sun is below the horizon, so a launch in the half hour after sunset leaves a trail that glows pink over steam already in shade. Within 46 pixels of a flame it takes the flame's orange, and near the pad at night the floodlights'.
- **Flames** (`flames`, `flame`): white-hot down the middle and orange outside, 36 pixels long at full size (18 on a landing burn), flickering a dozen times a second, with a glare of four flat rings round them that is stronger at night.
- **The countdown board** (`countdown`, `figures`): figures and letters three pixels by five. The top line names the rocket on the pad or next to come out (FALCON 9, F HEAVY, SATURN V…), because at three pixels wide a Titan and an Atlas are hard to tell apart. Under it, in amber, is the time to the next lift-off, adding up what is left of each stage at the pace in Settings, or the time since the last one for a minute and a half after it. It stops at T-00:10 through a hold.
- **A visiting flag** (`visitor`, `Flag`): a rocket from abroad has its country's flag run up the second pole, beside the Stars and Stripes, from its roll-out until its crawler is home again. It takes four seconds to go up or come down. Five fly one: Ariane 5 (France, as Dave named it), Soyuz (Russia), Long March 5 (China), H3 (Japan) and LVM3 (India), each nine pixels by six. Dave asked for it (see his decisions).
- **Night:** the pad's floodlights, with a pool of light on the mound; the hangar's door and windows; red lights on the tower, the water tower and the mast between the landing zones; white lights round the zones. A night launch lights its own steam.
- **No traffic and no high plane:** `cars` is empty, and nothing flies over a launch site.

## Time, live data and appearance
- **Time:** `now`: the real time, or today at the preview hour while Settings is previewing one. The hour for windows and traffic is the local clock hour.
- **Sun:** `skyPosition()` at `Location.shared` (low precision, about 1°). `night = smoothstep(4°, −8°, sun elevation)`.
- **Placement:** sky positions go onto the canvas looking along `heading()` (`place()`): toward the equator, or the compass point picked in Settings.
- **Network:** only the Airport, and only with its wind setting on (see The wind above). The other cities make no requests, the Spaceport included: its launches are its own. `Location.shared.start()` is called in `didMove`.
- **Appearance:** it ignores Light/Dark Mode. The real Sun already sets the look.

## Settings
`PixelCity.knobs`, read with `knob(_:)`. A change repaints at once (`settingsChanged`). A change of city hands over to a new scene of that city through a crossfade with both still running (0.8 s for a pick by hand, 4 s when it moves by itself), as Galaxy and Rain on Glass hand over; with no view to fade in, as in the render tests, it lays the same scene out again.
- **City** (`city.place`): Waterfront, Foothills, Long Bridge, Hillside Town, Overlook, Airport or Spaceport, as the index into `City`. Waterfront is the default. The key was `city.view` until the Street was cut from the front of the menu (0.78.0): under the old key every saved index would have meant the next city along, so the pick starts again at the default.
- **Move to another city automatically** (`city.shuffle`, off by default) and **Move every** (`city.shuffleMinutes`, 1 to 60 minutes, 10 by default). Dave asked for it on 2026-10-04: "keep waterfront as default, but add an option to auto shuffle between cities every x min, just like we have with like aurora and others".
  - **The wait is on the wall clock and saved** (`city.movedAt`, when the city last changed). `moveIfDue` checks it every 5 seconds and whenever a scene is built. Turning the option on, changing the minutes, or picking a city by hand starts it over.
  - **`moveOn`** picks a different city at random and saves it as `city.place`, exactly as Settings would: every display's copy of the scene follows, so they change together, and the City menu shows where we are.
  - **Why not count the time in the scene:** the first version did (an action started when the scene was built), and a side agent caught the flaw the same day. The app's own Shuffle, a relaunch and every rebuild make a new scene, so with Shuffle on a shorter interval the wait never finished and Pixel City came back on the same city every time. Now, if it has been away longer than the wait, it comes back as a new city.
- **Looking** (`city.looking`): Toward the midday Sun (the default: toward the equator, which keeps the Sun and Moon in the sky and the city backlit) or one of eight compass points. Dave asked for it on 2026-10-04 after reading that the Waterfront's fronts stay in cool shade at sunset: "lets add support for the sun direction perhaps". The Waterfront's flanks were laid out for the default, so looking east or west the Sun rises or sets behind downtown.
- **Flights by day** and **Flights at night** (`city.flightsDay`, 1× by default; `city.flightsNight`, 0.7×; both 0.25× to 3×): how busy the Airport is. Dave asked for them on 2026-10-04 ("sliders for like day time activity vs night time acticity"). A change shows at once: time on a stand and time away run down at the slider's pace, so a wait already under way speeds up or slows with it. (The first version fixed each wait's length when it began, and a side agent pointed out that a change then took up to 8 minutes to show.) At 3× the runway is the limit, since one aircraft has it at a time. After midnight only the night slider counts, unless a daytime hour is being previewed.
- **Land and take off into the real wind** (`city.wind`, on by default). The Airport's three settings sit in their own Airport section and show whatever the city, because a knob can only be hidden by a switch, not by the City menu. Dave asked for the live wind "as a toggle in the settings" (2026-10-04). Off, the Airport makes no requests and aircraft always go to the right.
- **A switch for each rocket** (`city.rocket.falcon9`, `.heavy`, `.starship`, `.sls`, `.shuttle`, `.saturn`, `.ariane`, `.titan`, `.atlas`, `.redstone`, `.soyuz`, `.longmarch`, `.h3`, `.lvm3`, all on by default), in the Spaceport section. Only the rockets switched on fly. With all of them off it flies them all, so the pad is never left empty.
- **Launches** (`city.launches`, 1× by default, 0.25× to 3×), in a Spaceport section that shows whatever the city, as the Airport's does. Time in the hangar and the count run down at this pace, and a change shows at once, on the countdown clock too. The machines keep their own speeds, so at 3× a lift-off comes a little over every two minutes and at 0.25× every ten to thirteen.
- **Preview a time of day** (`city.previewTime`, `city.previewHour`): shows today at that hour instead of now, the only way to see a sunset at noon. Off by default.

## Performance
Release build, 2x, 1512×982, measured 2026-10-04 with every city in place:
- **Per frame:** 0.45–0.49 ms CPU and 0.12–0.23 ms GPU for Foothills and the Overlook: a few dozen sprites. The four cities with water, measured 2026-10-05 with their moving things mirrored, are 0.52–0.67 ms CPU and 0.16–0.27 ms GPU (Waterfront 0.64–0.67 and 0.17–0.27, Long Bridge 0.52–0.62 and 0.16–0.20, Hillside Town 0.63–0.64 and 0.18–0.20, Airport 0.57–0.65 and 0.19–0.20). The extra over the 0.45–0.49 they cost before is the traffic's strip, repainted whenever a car moves a pixel, and the shader reading each thing's picture.
- **The 30 s repaint:** about 3 to 4.5 ms once (Waterfront 4.4, Foothills 4.2, Long Bridge 2.7–3.6, Hillside Town 3.6, Overlook 3.9, before its pigeons and traffic; the Airport wasn't timed). It loops over every art pixel in Swift for the sky, draws the city over a second buffer, and builds two `CGImage`s.
- **The Spaceport,** measured 2026-10-05 with a Falcon 9: 0.54 ms CPU and 0.22 ms GPU through a count, 0.74 to 0.75 and 0.26 to 0.42 with the lift-off's steam at its fullest (dusk and night), 0.72 and 0.50 during the climb, 0.57 and 0.25 as a booster lands. The steam is the cost: about a thousand puffs added into the density field 15 times a second. The big rockets raise more of it and leave a thicker trail: SLS and the Saturn V measured 1.04 to 1.10 ms CPU and 0.22 to 0.25 ms GPU at lift-off and in the climb. Two other readings that day came out at 1.35 ms CPU (one with 0.88 ms GPU), with other sessions' tests running on the machine. Starship measured 0.82 to 0.88 ms CPU and 0.21 ms GPU at lift-off and in the climb.
- Lots of headroom against the 2 ms budget.

## Gotchas and shortcuts
- **Its own maths.** `skyPosition()` and the Moon phase duplicate what `SkyMath` does better (`Sky.sun`, `Sky.moon`, `Sky.moonPhase`). The Moon can be about 5° off, since it has no lunar inclination, and its lit side isn't mirrored for the southern hemisphere.
- **Clock versus Sun.** The hour for windows and traffic is the local clock, while the Sun uses location, so a viewer far from their time zone's centre gets slightly mismatched light and windows.
- **`dt` is clamped** to 0–0.1 s in `update`, because the render harness once fed wildly negative steps.
- **Snapshots** use the fallback location: 40°N at the time zone's standard meridian, so the Sun can be minutes to about half an hour off from the viewer's real one.
- **Keep it crisp:** motion must stay rounded to whole art pixels, and new textures must use `.nearest`. The water's shader floors to art pixels itself, and samples its textures at the centre of a whole pixel: a texture handed to a shader is smoothed between pixels whatever its filtering mode, which once let a half-blended row of the far headland through as a dark line across the sea.
- **Seeded draws must not depend on the hour.** `drawTower` makes the same random draws in the same order on every repaint and only compares them with the hour, or the lights would reshuffle every 30 s.
- **Clouds and the high plane aren't mirrored** (`ponytail:` in the source). Their images would fall off the bottom of the water in three cities, and under the Dock on the Long Bridge. A boat or ship must not paint its own reflection any more: the water does it (the freighter and the boats did until 0.79.1).
- **The Sun often sets behind a building.** The flanks are low and half open, not empty, so on some days the last few degrees are hidden and only the glow shows. That's the real azimuth doing it.
- **Lights come on in a batch** every 30 s rather than one at a time.
- **The Airport's aircraft pitch no more than 1 in 6** (`ponytail:` in the source). Steeper, the slid columns turn a fuselage's stripes into a checkerboard: the first try went to 1 in 4 and looked like the Overlook's stray blocks. A real airliner climbs out steeper; drawing a climbing one by hand would allow it.
- **An aircraft's reflection goes by its height above its own ground,** not by its row on the screen. The airfield's strips are stacked up the screen far more than a low view would really show, so the true geometry (the ground in front of a thing hides the foot of its reflection) would leave a parked aircraft's image floating clear of the shore. The buildings' reflections take the same liberty.
- **The water's shader is built for the number of things it mirrors** (`waterShader(things:)`): one for the Waterfront, two for the Long Bridge, six for Hillside Town, five for the Airport, one for the Spaceport. Each is two uniforms, so Hillside Town's shader has 20, against the limit of about 30: a city with many more moving things should paint them into a strip, as the traffic is.
- **A mutable texture's rows run from the bottom,** the opposite of an image's (`Pixels.bytes(topDown:)`).
- **The Spaceport paints at the end of a frame** (`didFinishUpdate`), not in `update`. In a debug build a frame with the lift-off's steam in it takes up to 18 ms (the steam adds about 0.2 ms in a release build), so a test that stepped the round through by `update` took 80 seconds for twenty minutes of launches. Now `update` only moves things on, and `pixelCitySpaceportKeepsLaunching` paints every twentieth step.
- **`Bytes`, not `Pixels`, for the Spaceport's layer:** premultiplied bytes in a mutable texture's own order, painted with plain floats. `Pixels` would convert 71,000 pixels of vectors for every repaint.
- **Puffs let off together must live about as long as each other.** With lifetimes spread from 40 to 70 seconds, the last of a trail to go hung in the sky as separate round dots, which read as bubbles.
- **The wide lens isn't real.** A launch seen from five kilometres stays in a wide shot for minutes and never gets small enough to matter; at the size these rockets are drawn it would leave the top of the picture in 14 seconds, still full size. The squeeze is there so the rocket can dwindle and its trail can arc.
- **A scene's first rocket has already rolled out,** so `pad.x` has to start at the full distance. It started at 0 until the second day's work, and the first strongback of every scene vanished at the end of the climb instead of rolling home. Nobody had noticed; it showed up as a visiting flag coming down too early.
- **A small cloud has to stay close in.** The small rockets' lift-off steam is a third of a Falcon's. Thrown out at the same speed it came apart into separate round puffs, like popcorn; it's now thrown a third as far.
- **The trail is laid back along the rocket's axis,** where its flame ends. The first build laid it straight below the rocket's foot, which is the same place only while the rocket is upright: once it leaned, the flame pointed down to the left and the smoke began below and to the right of it. Dave caught it the same day (see his decisions).
- **The first climb leaned too soon,** from 50 pixels up, and went across the top of the tower. It leans from 70.
- **`pixelCitySwitchesCity` counts nodes** to see that each city built, and wanted more than 10. The Spaceport has 10, because its moving things share one. The test now wants more than 8.
- **Aircraft come down low.** Seen from the side, a real approach is nearly flat, so a landing arrives in front of the hangars and the terminal rather than out of the sky. Take-offs are the ones that use the sky.

## Dave's feedback and decisions
- It was built in the first batch of scenes ("build out all of those ideas") and not touched again until October.
- He wants every live scene to follow his real location and time. This one does, through the Sun's position, not just the clock.
- **2026-10-04, design review.** He asked for "a deep dive design review… to push it to the next level of fidelity". The review rendered eleven times of day and found: the Sun dropped behind the Street's solid far row 1.5 to 3.5 hours before sunset and was never seen low; light had no direction; every building was the same box; at 21:00 about 68% of windows were lit, all alike; the traffic sat under the Dock and the top 40% was empty sky; and nothing moved between repaints but cars, clouds, the beacon and a plane. Shown a ranked list, he said "proceed based on your own recommendation", which was: light from the real Sun, a waterfront composition and a kit of building kinds, as a second choice in Settings to compare with the Street. That's the Waterfront. The smaller faults were fixed for both cities first (headlights, the Sun's halo, the Moon's size, stars through clouds).
- **Waterfront stays the default** (2026-10-04, once all six were in), with the automatic move between cities as an option beside it.
- **The Overlook's first version wasn't good enough** (2026-10-04): "there are stray moving white blocks that on overlapping buildings, and there doesnt seem to be much life to the city, lets fix it up or remove it". The blocks were its steam, which had only been checked in still frames. It was fixed rather than cut (see Life, above). The same day he named Foothills, Long Bridge and Hillside Town "the best so far".
- **A menu of cities.** While the Waterfront was being built he asked for "other 'pixel city' backdrops entirely… 1 or 2 more 'cities' that can be toggled in the settings, maybe like a sweeping city landscape", so City became a menu to add to.
- **The Street was cut** (2026-10-04, 0.78.0). It was the original city: side-on from the kerb, a near and a far row of plain blocks with a grid of square windows. Dave: "we should consider removing 'street' too since its so similar to waterfront and waterfront is better, what do you think". Agreed, and done: the Waterfront has the same street, traffic and lamps in front of it, and answers everything the design review found wrong with the Street (the Sun never seen low, light with no direction, one repeated box, traffic under the Dock). Keeping it also meant keeping a second, older way of drawing the sky, placing the Sun and lighting a wall. It's in git history before that commit.
- **The Airport, 2026-10-04.** Asked for with "a pixel airport would be really cool, with planes taking off and landing, taxing etc", held until the conversation was compacted, then: "spin up a research agent to select what a good airport style would work well for this and add a pixel airport option to the pixel city selections. ask me any clarifying questions along the way".
  - **The research** (its notes are summarised here; its messages were held until the build's turn ended, so they were read from its transcript meanwhile): of the side-on views it compared, it picked a shoreline airfield seen across water through the middle of the runway, so every aircraft stays in profile and every turn happens off screen, with the view from the terminal roof as runner-up (better sprites, but the tower leaves the picture, moving aircraft fall under 20 pixels and a large still foreground repeats the Overlook's first fault). It blocked the pick in at this canvas size to test it. The first build had been started while it worked and had reached the same view.
  - **What it found, and what was done with it:** an airliner needs 26 pixels to read as one and is comfortable at 34 (ours are 42); what carries it is a swept fin in one flat colour, a long white fuselage whose underside rises to the tail, a dark engine under the wing and windows that light at night; never rotate the sprite, slide it; keep the runway to about a third of real speed (taken: the first build was faster). Lights, read by the agent in FAA AIM 2-1 and 4-3-23 and 14 CFR 25.1385 and 25.1401: runway edges white, taxiway edges blue, a civil land airport's beacon white and green alternately at 24 to 30 flashes a minute, aircraft red on the left wing and green on the right with a red beacon whenever engines run. It advised against approach flashers, PAPI and pavement lights as noise at this size, and against live flight data (a network call that gives away where you are). Wind: AIM 4-3-6 has the runway follow the wind at 5 knots or more; a windsock is fully out at 15 knots (AC 150/5345-27E, which it read by way of Wikipedia). Its speeds and turnaround figures came from search summaries, not sources.
  - **Its references,** for study only, licences as it read them: Wikimedia Commons photographs of Boston Logan across the water (CC BY 4.0), Las Vegas (CC BY-SA 2.0), Salzburg at sunrise (GFDL) and Zürich at night (CC BY-SA 3.0); OpenGameArt's London Luton Airport scene (CC-BY 3.0) and a pixel 767 (CC-BY 4.0). Nothing was copied: every aircraft and building is drawn in code.
  - **Dave's answers** to three questions: the live wind, "but as a toggle in the settings"; "Calm regional" for how busy (a movement about once a minute) over a busy hub or a quiet airfield; and "Across the bay" kept over an inland airfield behind a fence or mountains behind.
  - **Night was too still.** The first build shared the road traffic's hourly table, and a side agent pointed out that around midnight a parked aircraft then waited 17 to 37 minutes. Dave: "i dont think we need to be so realistic at night, if day time is just a couple minutes, then lets have night time be like 4-6 minutes, it needs to not be just still most of the time", then "or just add sliders to the settings for this, sliders for like day time activity vs night time acticity, whatever hyou think". Both: two sliders, with night's default giving about 3.5 to 8 minutes on a stand.
  - **Reflections:** "when planes take off and come in for a landing, can you show reflections in the water (as realisticly positioned as possible)". Done in the water's own shader, for every aircraft (see Reflections in the bay). Then, on 2026-10-05: "can you make sure all of the pixel cities that have water have those same reflections, they look amazing". Every city with water now mirrors its moving things the same way (see Moving things in the water).
- **The Spaceport, 2026-10-05.** His request: "this next one is going to sound crazy but I want you to build a pixel rocket launch site, from the 'spectators at a safe distance' view that we often see from nasa footage going back to shuttle launch and even saturn V from where the public could watch (IE a far but not too far distance)". He listed: a "modern falcon 9 launch (maybe even support for super heavy or starship)"; a "falcon 9 return landing (or double falcon 9s for super heavy)"; "cranes carry landed rockets away"; "new rockets get brough out and set on launch pads"; "the process repeats with 'random' rockets (we can only have so many, like 2 or 3)"; a "day night cycle like real life or let user pin to a time like we do in other settings"; and a "pixel cool launch and cool pixel smoke trail but static camera, not a tracking camera, so we want a wide shot".
  - **His answers to three questions:** a city in Pixel City, over a wallpaper of its own; Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy first, with Starship to follow ("double falcon 9s" was read as Falcon Heavy, whose two side boosters land side by side); and for following real launches from a public schedule, "No, keep it self-contained".
  - **Mock-ups first,** as he offered ("feel free to run some design images by me if you want before you get too far"): six frames painted in Python over real renders of the empty city's sky and lagoon (roll-out with a booster under the crane, lift-off in late afternoon, the climb at sunset, a Falcon Heavy's two boosters landing at night, a Falcon Heavy on the pad at dusk, and the lift-off again with open water). He picked the spectators' bank over open water or a bank with only the clock and flag, the distance as shown over closer or farther, and the rocket shrinking and arcing over one that stays full size and leaves by the top. Then: "all of your mockups are amazing, i cant wait to see it".
  - **No research agent this time.** The real figures here (a Falcon 9's size, seven seconds to clear the tower, eight minutes to a landing) are from memory and weren't checked against sources. Nothing was copied: every rocket and building is drawn in code, and none carries a name or a logo.
  - **Falcon Heavy came up too seldom.** The first build picked each rocket at random with a Heavy one time in five, and always began a scene with a plain Falcon 9. A side agent pointed out the same day that a ten-minute visit then had about one chance in three of the double landing Dave had asked for. Rockets now come from a shuffled set of three with one Heavy in it, the first of a scene included.
  - **His verdict on the first build:** "its near perfect, the only tweak id love to see is if we can better align the rocket and the plume at higher altitudes, it looks a bit offset from the smoke plume", with a screenshot of a leaning rocket whose trail began to the right of its flame. Fixed the same day (see Gotchas).
  - **More rockets, switches and a flag (the same day).** Dave: "now that we have the core concept working really well can you: add some more popular rockets (only the ones that can come back for landing should), so like lets add sls but we wont see a landing comeback etc; allow the user to toggle which rockets they want to see launched in the settings; whatever else you think could make this cooler, maybe even launching older rockets, like the mercury redstone, atlas gemini, and saturn V, maybe even shuttle, let me know what you think". While it was being built: "bonus points for popping up country of origin flag next to USA when its a non US rocket, like the arianne should pop up a french flag next to US during that launch", "most rockets will be US based so this wont be often but its a nice tip of the hat to the country of origin", and "this is fast becoming my new favorite wallpaper of them all, watching the rocket launches is so cool".
    - **What was built:** SLS, the Saturn V, the Space Shuttle, Gemini-Titan, Mercury-Atlas and Mercury-Redstone ("atlas gemini" was read as both), and Ariane 5 for the flag; a switch each; the rocket's name on the countdown board; and the big four rolling out standing on a crawler. These were choices made for him, not asked: that rockets which land and rockets which don't take turns, that the three small ones are drawn taller than true, and that the Shuttle is seen side on and doesn't come back.
  - **Rockets from abroad (the same day).** Offered three next steps (Starship, the Shuttle's orbiter gliding home, and Soyuz, Long March, H3 and LVM3 with their flags, "which countries' flags go on your wallpaper is your call"), Dave said: "push it then go ahead with you ideas on what to do next". All four went in, each with its switch and its flag, on by default like the rest. Their colours and sizes are from memory; the Soyuz is the older grey and orange one.
  - **Starship (the same day),** the second step agreed at the start, built when Dave said "go ahead with you ideas on what to do next". It has its own pad left of the ramp, where the tank farm stood (the tanks moved to the pad's other side). What's real: the tower lifts the rocket on and catches the booster in its arms. What isn't: a real booster and ship are stacked separately.
  - **The ship's return (the same day).** Dave: "can you have it so the starship booster comes back to chopstick catch and then a bit later the starship top piece comes back for a ground landing". It lands on a landing zone, after the belly-flop and flip of the real test flights, and the crane takes it. Between asking and seeing it: "everything is incredible".
  - **One order for every screen.** The order was first kept by the class, shared by every copy of the scene. A side agent pointed out that two displays drawing from it in turn could leave one with all the landings and the other with none. Each scene now has its own.
  - **The beacon showed through the smoke.** Dave: "minor ui issue, the blinking tower light appears on top of the smoke but the smoke obscures the tower, the smoke should also obscure the light". The beacon was drawn above every city's moving things; at the Spaceport it now sits just behind the layer the smoke is in.
  - **The orbiter's landing (the same day),** the third of the ideas Dave said to go ahead with. Before it, "only the ones that can come back for landing should" left the Shuttle with nothing coming back.
  - **What differs from his list:** a crane carries each booster away hanging from its hook, as he said, though a real one is laid on a transporter; and rockets are brought out lying down and stood up, as real ones are, not set on the pad by a crane.
- **More cities, 2026-10-04.** A research agent ranked eight backdrops by how good each would look against how hard it is to draw in code: Foothills, Overlook (down over rooftops to a far skyline), Long Bridge (a suspension bridge across a bay), Hillside Town (houses up a slope above a harbour), Park (trees that follow the season), Old Town (a clock tower showing the real time), Desert City and Elevated Railway. It advised against a neon alley (flashy, hides the sky, hardest to generate), a canal city (the Waterfront again) and a snowy town (better as live-weather snow everywhere), and found no openly licensed art good enough or the right size to ship, so each is drawn in code. Shown the top four, Dave picked all of them: Foothills, Overlook, Long Bridge and Hillside Town. Overlook carries a risk the agent flagged: its middle distance of roofs may turn to noise at this size.
