# Pixel Spaceport

A pixel-art launch site that never stops, seen across a lagoon from the bank the public watches from. Eighteen rockets, from Mercury-Redstone to Starship and New Glenn, roll out, lift off on a column of smoke and arc away. The Falcons' boosters fly back and land, and a crane carries them off; Starship's booster is caught by its tower and its ship lands a little later; the Shuttle's orbiter glides home to a runway. It all happens under the real Sun and Moon, and is mirrored in the lagoon. With Follow real launches on, the pad is cleared for each real launch of a rocket it draws, counted down to the real T−0.

- **Files:** `Sources/Atrium/PixelCity.swift`. It is Pixel City's scene, pinned to its launch site: the sky, the Sun and Moon, the stars and clouds, the light on things and the water's shader are all Pixel City's, and [pixel-city.md](pixel-city.md) is where they are written up.
- **Entry:** `pixelSpaceport(size:)` builds `PixelCity(size:spaceport: true)`. Its entry in Scenes.swift is "Pixel Spaceport", icon `location.north.fill` (there is no rocket among the system's symbols), tint `.orange`, with `PixelCity.spaceportKnobs`.
- **Kind:** SpriteKit on Pixel City's low-resolution canvas (240 art pixels to the screen's height). The far shore and our bank are painted into the backdrop every 30 s; everything that moves is painted into one layer.
- **Its own wallpaper since 0.90.0.** It began on 2026-10-05 as the Spaceport, a seventh city in Pixel City's City menu, and was moved out the same day (see Dave's decisions). In the code it is still a case of `City`, one that `City.menu` leaves out, and `spaceport` on the scene says which wallpaper it is: that picks the settings (`launchKnobs` or `cityKnobs`, read through `knob(_:)`) and switches off the move between cities.

## How it works
Dave asked for it on 2026-10-05 (his words are under his decisions below), and picked the view, the distance and the climb from six mock-ups painted over real renders of the sky and lagoon.

- **Composition,** bottom to top:
  - **Our bank:** grass in shadow along the bottom 6 to 9 rows, with the countdown board on the left, the Stars and Stripes and a second pole for a visiting flag (each with a ground light at its foot that comes on after dark), thirteen people and a camera on a tripod in silhouette, and a palm at the right edge. The Dock hides the middle of it, so the board and the flags are to the left.
  - **A lagoon,** the bottom 23.5%, which mirrors everything across it.
  - **The far shore:** shell sand, a road, scrub and clumps of low trees, cleared round the buildings. Right of the pad the road is a runway: paler, with lights along it after dark.
  - **The site,** left to right: a tall blank assembly building far off in the haze; the hangar, a long white shed with a door at the pad end, lit inside after dark; Starship's pad, a launch mount on legs beside a dark lattice tower 96 rows tall, the tallest thing here; the pad, a mound 6 rows high with a ramp on the hangar's side, the flame trench's mouth, a launch mount, a lattice tower 58 rows tall with an arm and a mast (the beacon is on top), a lightning mast either side, two floodlights and a water tower; two tanks and the liquid oxygen's sphere; and two landing zones, 77% and 89% of the way across, with a hut and a mast between them.
  - **The sky** is three quarters of the picture, for the climb and the trail.
- **Everything that moves is painted into one layer** (`launchLayer`, a mutable texture as wide as the canvas that covers everything above the water, painted by `paintLaunch` into a `Bytes` canvas): the smoke, the glare round each flame, the strongback, the rocket, the flames, the boosters and the crane. Two reasons: smoke has to pass in front of some things and behind others, and the lagoon's shader can then mirror the lot as one thing (see Moving things in the water). It's repainted only when something has moved a pixel (`launchShown`).
- **Rockets** (`Rocket`, each with a `Kit`; drawn by `stamp`): each is drawn standing, as rows of letters. A Falcon 9 is 46 rows tall with a body 3 pixels wide and a fairing of 5: about a metre and a half to the pixel, and half as slender as the real thing, which is about 70 m by 3.7 m and would be 2 pixels wide. None carries lettering or a logo. Real sizes here are from memory, not checked.

  - **The three from the 1960s are drawn a fifth to a third taller than true,** and as wide as a Falcon: a Mercury-Redstone to scale would be 17 rows by 1, a line. Electron is drawn half as tall again as true for the same reason (18 m would be 12 rows). The rest are to the Falcon's scale, so a Saturn V stands as tall as the pad's tower and its mast.
  - **What else differs** (`Kit`): how long and wide the flame is (14 by 2 pixels for the Redstone, 52 by 7 for the Saturn V, 44 by 9 for SLS); how thick the trail is (the Shuttle's and SLS's solid boosters leave one 1.7 times a Falcon 9's); how much steam the lift-off raises (the small three raise a third as much, and it stays close in so it holds together as a cloud); how briskly it climbs (the Saturn V at 0.82 of a Falcon's pace, the Shuttle at 1.15); how far it leans over as it climbs (`arc`: the four drawn taller than the lens's 60 true-to-size rows, Starship, the Saturn V, New Glenn and SLS, half as far as the rest, since at their size a Falcon's turn looked abrupt; the Mercury-Redstone a third as far by its own `lean`, since it never went for orbit); whether it sheds cold vapour on the pad (the Titan's fuels weren't cold); and whether it sheds strap-on boosters in the climb, and how many (`sheds`: eight of them, below).
  - **Which rocket** (`nextRocket`): only those switched on in Settings fly. Rockets whose boosters come back (the two Falcons and Starship) and rockets that fly once take turns, so there is a landing after every other lift-off however many are switched on. Within each kind every rocket flies once, in a shuffled order, before any flies again, so with all of them on a Falcon Heavy's double landing, like Starship's catch, comes every sixth launch. Each scene keeps its own order (`hangar`), so two displays each get their turns of both kinds; a new scene takes up where the last one to pick left off, with the same rockets still to fly in a fresh order, so the turns carry across the app's Shuffle and a move between cities. A rocket is picked when its wait in the hangar begins, so the board can name it; if it has been switched off by the time it would roll out, another is picked. Half the time a Falcon's boosters have flown before and are sooty toward the foot.
  - **Turning and shrinking** (`stamp`): each pixel under the rocket looks up the art it falls on, so one drawing serves lying on the transporter, standing up, leaning in the climb and shrinking with height. Mid-raise it's jagged, as any turned pixel art is.
  - **Light** (`rocketPaint`): each paint (white, a tank's orange foam, bare metal, cream) has a lit side, a face and a shaded side, from the same Sun as the buildings; after dark the floodlights have it on the pad, by degrees (`floodlit`): fully at the mount and not at all 80 pixels off (about the tower's height, ten seconds of climb at a Falcon's pace), so it rolls into them, climbs out of them, and Starship's booster comes back down into them and rides home out of them; a landing zone's lights do the same for a booster landing there, at 60% of the pad's; in the air its foot takes the flame's light.
- **The round** (`launch`, `Pad.Phase`), three and a half to four and a half minutes from one lift-off to the next:
  - **In the hangar** for 50 to 110 seconds.
  - **Roll-out:** a Falcon, or one of the small three, lies on its strongback, foot first, and rolls out of the hangar's door at 3.5 pixels a second, along the shore and up the ramp (30 seconds). What is still inside the door isn't painted. The four big ones come out standing, as theirs did, on a crawler that comes in from the left edge (the assembly building's side) at the same speed, past the hangar and up the ramp (54 seconds).
  - **The raise:** 18 seconds from lying to standing, hinged at the launch mount. The strongback is as long as most of the rocket, so a Redstone's is short. A rocket that came out standing only settles for 4 seconds.
  - **The count:** 70 seconds of fuelling, with cold vapour drifting off the rocket. The strongback leans back for the last 20.
  - **Lift-off:** the engines light 2.6 seconds early; the deluge's steam rolls out of both ends of the flame trench and up round the mount for ten seconds; the strongback is thrown back, and lowered once the rocket has gone. The flame is hidden below the pad's deck (`deck` in `flames`), where the trench takes it: at ignition only the four rows between the engines and the deck show, and the rest comes into view as the rocket climbs, fully clear about five seconds up. Starship's mount has no trench, so its flame fills the space under the mount down to the ground, as at Starbase; landing burns stop at the ground.
  - **The climb** (`ascent`, `lens`), 21 seconds: see below.
  - **Roll-back:** the empty strongback, or the crawler, goes home at 5 pixels a second, and the wait in the hangar starts again.
- **Starship's round** differs, because it has its own pad (`Kit.caught`, `starX`, `site`):
  - **It rolls in standing** on a crawler from the left edge, as the big NASA rockets do, but stops under its tower, short of the ramp.
  - **The tower's arms lift it** onto the launch mount, 9 rows up, in ten seconds. The arms are a carriage on the tower and a beam out across the rocket, just under the booster's grid fins; they rest low, where a crawler leaves a rocket, and are painted with the things that move.
  - **It lifts off** like the rest, with half as much steam again as a Falcon 9 and a thin trail.
  - **Its booster comes back to the tower** 62 seconds later (`Booster` with `zone` 2): the same fall and landing burn as a Falcon's, with no legs, down to the arms, which hold it over the mount. It doesn't come straight down (`pose(caught:)`): it falls 26 columns out from the arms, slides in toward them through its burn, foot first and leaning back along its path by up to about 20 degrees, and straightens as it comes between them, as a real one does.
  - **The pad waits for it** (`Pad.Phase.recover`). After 14 seconds in the arms it is lowered onto the crawler, ten seconds more, and rides home off the left edge. No crane is needed, and no other rocket rolls out until it has gone.
  - **Then its ship comes back** (`Booster.ship`, `pose(ship:)`), 100 seconds after lift-off, to one of the two landing zones: so a Starship's count holds, like a Falcon's, until one is free. It falls on its belly, nose to the right and its black tiles down, at 22 pixels a second, slower than a booster, swooping in from the left the way its nose points (`swoop`: 24 × ((z − 40) / 220)^1.5 columns out, about 35 where it comes into view and 3 as the flip begins, so the slant steepens to vertical and it lands over its zone). At 100 pixels up its engines light and it swings upright over the next 45, then comes down on its flame and stands on its skirt, about 20 seconds after it came into view. It is the ship's half of Starship's drawing, turned by `stamp`. The crane carries it off as it does a Falcon's booster, with its hook two rows higher.
- **The Shuttle's engines and its roll** (`shuttleStack`, `roll`, the Shuttle's case in `flames`). Its drawing is built from three parts, the orbiter, the tank and a booster, 20 columns wide with the tank's axis up the middle (so it stands with the tank and boosters over the mount and the orbiter hanging off to the left, as the real one did). Its three main engines light 6.6 seconds before lift-off and burn a short, pale flame (16 rows, 2 wide, white to blue at 60% alpha: `pale` in `flame`) under the orbiter's tail, three rows above the boosters' nozzles; the two solid boosters light at zero, at once, each a bright flame 40 rows long and 3 wide, one behind the other. Then the roll programme: between 8 and 13 seconds (at a Falcon's pace), as it clears the tower, the stack rolls half a turn about the tank. In cross-section the orbiter's columns lie along a radius and swing from the tank's left round behind it (hidden) to its right, mirrored, while the boosters, one in front and one hidden behind, swing out to the tank's two sides and back, their flames with them; parts are laid down farthest first. It ends heads down, the orbiter on the underside as it arcs over to the right, which is how the real one flew. A second later the boosters fall away (above) and only the pale flame burns on. The real roll was about 120° and took until T+20; ours is half a turn so the side-on drawing ends mirrored, and finishes before the (much earlier) separation.
- **The Shuttle's orbiter comes home** (`Orbiter`), 150 seconds after a Shuttle's lift-off. It's the Shuttle's own drawing of the orbiter, laid on its belly with its nose to the left (`orbiterArt`, the same rows turned the other way and stamped a quarter turn over), so no second drawing was needed.
  - **The glide:** in from beyond the right edge at 16 pixels a second, 62 rows up, nose down and sinking at 11, which is steep, as a real one's glide was.
  - **The flare:** below 9 rows it sinks more gently and lifts its nose, and its wheels are down.
  - **The roll-out:** it touches down near the right-hand landing zone and slows at 3 pixels a second each second behind a red and white drag chute, stopping after about 43 pixels.
  - **Then** it stands for 12 seconds and a tug tows it off by the tail, the way it came, at 4 pixels a second. A real one was towed nose first.
  - **The runway is the shore road,** in front of the landing zones, so the orbiter is drawn at full size and passes in front of whatever stands on them. A crane working there at the same moment would overlap it; at the usual pace they miss each other by a minute.
- **A real launch** (`liveLaunch`, `Pad.live`), with Follow real launches on: the soonest launch in the schedule of a rocket drawn here whose T−0 is inside the next 10 minutes, or passed within the last minute (In Flight), or is On Hold. The feed's vehicle names map to rockets by prefix (`LaunchSchedule.kind`): any Long March flies as the Long March 5 and any GSLV or PSLV as the LVM3, a Falcon 9 whose mission says Dragon, CRS or Crew carries the capsule, and rockets not drawn (Nuri, Vega-C, Kuaizhou, Atlas V...) are left to the feed. When one is inside the window, the rocket waiting in the hangar leaves at once as that rocket; a random rocket already rolling out or counting flies first, and the round is short enough that the window covers it, though a window that opens with a random rocket just fuelled can put the real lift-off a minute or two late. On the pad its count is the wall clock's to the real T−0 (`LaunchSchedule.now`; Settings' Launches pace doesn't apply), and every display counts the same clock, so they lift off together. The board counts the real time from the roll-out and swaps the rocket's name for LIVE every three seconds, through the count and the minute and a half after lift-off. While the feed says On Hold the count holds, frozen where it was and never inside the last ten seconds (where the engines light: the first render of a hold had the deluge running), and the board says HOLD. Held past 20 minutes, slipped out of the window or gone from the feed, the board says SCRUB and the rocket goes back unflown: lowered onto its strongback (`Pad.Phase.lower`, the raise in reverse) and rolled back into the hangar, or standing on its crawler if it came that way; the random round goes on, and that T−0 (`Pad.passed`) isn't taken up again unless the feed moves it. The window is 10 minutes because a rocket needs about two from the hangar to the end of its count, and a fuelled rocket held for half an hour looks stuck (Dave chose 10 over 30 or a slider). The feed's statuses lag (Nuri's success on 2026-10-07 was recorded two hours after lift-off), so lift-off keys off the T−0, never the status, and a hold called in the last minute can be missed, which Dave accepted.
- **The climb.** Height is `0.65 t² (1 + t / 40)`, so it clears the tower in about 9 seconds (a real one takes about 7, from memory). The sky is seen through a wide lens (`lens`): true to size for the first 60 pixels up, then squeezed by a logarithm, with everything drawn smaller by the same amount. So the rocket shrinks to a spark and leans over to the right as it climbs, leaving a trail that tapers and arcs; it's in view for about 18 seconds, where at full size it would be out of the top of the picture in 14. Dave picked this over full size and straight up. It only starts to lean once it's above the tower. The Mercury-Redstone leans a third as far (`Kit.lean`), so it climbs steep and short: its real flights were ballistic arcs, up to about 116 miles and down about 300 miles out, not the long pitch-over of a rocket going for orbit. The biggest rockets lean half as far (`Kit.arc`: any drawn taller than the 60 rows the lens shows true to size, which is Starship at 81 rows, the Saturn V at 73, New Glenn at 65 and SLS at 64, where a Falcon 9 is 46 and the Shuttle stack 37): at their size the full turn looked abrupt (see Dave's decisions). A new rocket that tall gets the gentler arc without being told.
- **Strap-on boosters falling away** (`shed`): eight rockets drop theirs 14 seconds (at a Falcon's pace) into the climb, near the top of the picture, as the rocket shrinks to a spark. The real ones go at about two minutes: the Shuttle's and SLS's solids at T+2:04 and 2:12, Ariane 5's at 2:20, H3's at 1:56, LVM3's at 2:12, Soyuz's four liquid strap-ons at 1:58, Long March 5's four at 2:53, and a Falcon Heavy's two at about 2:30. The rocket flies on drawn without them, with a narrower flame and half the trail; the boosters coast on up its path at the speed they fell away at, slowing by 2 pixels a second each second, so they fall behind the core, and leave by the top of the picture a few seconds after it. The climb phase lasts 25 seconds for these instead of 21, so they're gone before the strongback rolls back.
  - **The pictures** come from the rocket's own rows (`strapOns`): its core is the drawing with the outer columns blanked, and one booster is those columns cut out, from the booster's nose down, so no second drawing is needed (SLS's hand-drawn pair came out byte for byte the same and went). The Shuttle's are `shuttleStack(roll: .pi, boosters: false)` and `shuttleBoosterArt`, since its stack is assembled; a Falcon Heavy's are a Falcon 9 and `heavySide`, and they keep their soot if the sides had flown before.
  - **Two** (the Shuttle, SLS, Ariane 5, H3, LVM3, Falcon Heavy) drift apart, 4 columns each side and then 6 a second at the lens's scale, and tumble outward at 0.3 radians a second. The Shuttle's two are drawn one each side though, seen side on, its real ones would have gone toward and away from the viewer.
  - **Four** (Soyuz, Long March 5) spread into the Korolev cross: a pair to either side of the point they left, 0.7 of that spread each way, and a pair as far again behind, each swinging over two and a half seconds to point nose-first out along its diagonal. A true cross seen from the side would put two of them beyond the nose, which read wrong in the first frames, so the cross trails the core instead: from the ground the real one is seen from behind, with the core's plume at its centre. Drawn with two boosters showing, these rockets shed four, as the real ones do: the two that were hidden in front and behind come out.
  - **None comes back but the Heavy's.** They're drawn from the pad's state alone, never as `Booster`s. A Falcon Heavy's two are also the `Booster`s that come in at the top 62 seconds after lift-off to land, which already existed: only the visible split is new.
- **Boosters** (`Booster`): 62 seconds after lift-off (a real one takes about 8 minutes) the booster comes back in at the top over a landing zone, falling at 34 pixels a second with its grid fins out; from 170 pixels up its landing burn slows it at whatever rate stops it as it touches; its legs come down in the last 26; dust rolls out along the ground. A Falcon Heavy's two side boosters, seen falling away in the climb (above), come back 1.3 seconds apart, one to each zone; its centre core flies on.
- **The crane** (`Crane`): 14 seconds after a landing a crawler crane drives in from the right at 5 pixels a second, lowers its hook to the booster's top, lifts it a little (its legs fold) and carries it off the way it came at 4. With two boosters down it takes the nearer one first and comes back.
- **A count holds at ten seconds** while a booster from the last flight is still standing on a landing zone the next one needs (a Falcon Heavy needs both).
- **Smoke** (`Puff`, `puff()`, `paintSmoke`): four kinds of puff, all the same thing with different numbers: vapour off a fuelled rocket, the deluge's steam, the trail (laid from where the flame ended a moment ago to where it ends now, which is 30 pixels back along the rocket's own axis at full size, and smaller the higher it's laid), and a landing's dust.
  - **It moves in steps of a fifteenth of a second,** as hand-drawn smoke would: each puff slows, swells toward its full size, rises if it's big, and drifts on a wind that blows the way the clouds go and harder at some heights than others, which is what kinks a trail.
  - **How long it lasts:** the lift-off's steam 36 to 44 seconds, the trail 46 to 54 at its foot and about half that at its top, a landing's dust 9. Steam rises while it's warm, for its first 14 seconds, and then hangs over the pad. (At first the steam lasted 16 to 21 seconds and the trail up to 60: see Dave's decisions.)
  - **Painting:** every puff adds a round hump to a field of density. Where that's thick enough there is cloud, in three flat tones: a rim toward the light where the cloud is much thinner two pixels that way, shade where it's much thinner the other way, the body between. "Much thinner" is by ratio, so the tones follow a thick cloud's lobes; by difference, half of every cloud fell into shade. Thin edges are part clear (40% and 72% solid).
  - **Its light:** the Sun's and the sky's by day. After sunset, what is high enough is still in sunlight: the Earth's shadow climbs 26 rows for each degree the Sun is below the horizon, so a launch in the half hour after sunset leaves a trail that glows pink over steam already in shade. Within 46 pixels of a flame it takes the flame's orange, and near the pad at night the floodlights', at any stage of the round: a soft glow, at most 60% of their white, fading to nothing 40 pixels out. A landing zone's lights do the same for a landing's dust, at 45% and 30 pixels, so it doesn't go dark the moment the engine cuts.
- **Flames** (`flames`, `flame`): white-hot down the middle and orange outside, 36 pixels long at full size (18 on a landing burn), flickering a dozen times a second, with a glare of four flat rings round them that is stronger at night.
- **The countdown board** (`countdown`, `figures`): figures and letters three pixels by five. The top line names the rocket on the pad or next to come out (FALCON 9, F HEAVY, SATURN V…), because at three pixels wide a Titan and an Atlas are hard to tell apart. Under it, in amber, is the time to the next lift-off, adding up what is left of each stage at the pace in Settings, or the time since the last one for a minute and a half after it. While it counts up, the name is the rocket that just flew (`flew`): the next one is picked when the strongback is home, about 40 seconds after lift-off, and for a day the board named it over the old flight's T+ (see Dave's decisions). It stops at T-00:10 through a hold.
- **A visiting flag** (`visitor`, `Flag`): a rocket from abroad has its country's flag run up the second pole, beside the Stars and Stripes, from its roll-out until its crawler is home again. It takes four seconds to go up or come down. Five fly one: Ariane 5 (France, as Dave named it), Soyuz (Russia), Long March 5 (China), H3 (Japan) and LVM3 (India), each nine pixels by six. Dave asked for it (see his decisions).
- **Night:** the pad's floodlights, with a pool of light on the mound; the hangar's door and windows; red lights on the tower, the water tower and the mast between the landing zones; white lights round the zones; a ground light at each flag pole's foot, warm and small, so the flags can still be made out (`cloth`: the cloth and the poles take its light instead of going black). A night launch lights its own steam.
- **No traffic and no high plane:** `cars` is empty, and nothing flies over a launch site.

## Time, live data and appearance
- **Time and the Sun** are Pixel City's: the real time, or today at the preview hour; the Sun and Moon from `Location.shared`.
- **Network:** none unless Follow real launches is on (off by default). Then `LaunchSchedule.shared` (LaunchSchedule.swift) asks Launch Library 2 by The Space Devs for the next eight launches that are Go, On Hold or In Flight, and keeps those with a T−0 known to the minute (the feed ignores a precision filter in the request; a launch known to the hour would clear the pad for nothing): one request of about 4 KB, about once an hour and then 12, 3 and 1 minutes before the next launch of a rocket drawn here, so at most four or five an hour (seven with two launches in the hour) against the free limit of 15 an hour per address, which every Mac behind one router shares (a side agent raised this; two Macs fit). One fetch is shared by every display and the Settings preview. A 429 is backed off for as long as the reply says, keeping the last list, so a count already running still ends at the T−0 last seen. Nothing about the user goes in the request. The scene asks the schedule every 15 s of wall clock from `update` (it fetches only when due), so polling stops while the wallpaper is hidden or another is up; the switch is cached in `following` at repaints, like Launches, so no setting is read every frame.
- **Appearance:** it ignores Light/Dark Mode. The real Sun already sets the look.

## Settings
`PixelCity.spaceportKnobs`. A change repaints at once.
- **Launches** (`spaceport.launches`, 1× by default, 0.25× to 3×). Time in the hangar and the count run down at this pace, and a change shows at once, on the countdown clock too. (A value written from outside the app, with `defaults write`, is taken up within 30 seconds: see Performance.) The machines keep their own speeds, so at 3× a lift-off comes a little over every two minutes and at 0.25× every ten to thirteen.
- **Follow real launches** (`spaceport.live`, off by default), in the Launches section. In the ten minutes before a real lift-off of a rocket drawn here, the pad is that rocket's (A real launch, above). Under it, while on, the status line (`spaceport.status`) names the next launch the Spaceport will follow (`Next: Falcon 9 Block 5 · Starlink Group 15-25 · 10 Oct 2026, 7:00 PM · Launch Library 2 by The Space Devs · updated 9:12 AM`), or says none has a time yet, or that the feed couldn't be reached or has had its 15 requests; Refresh Now fetches at once, at most every two minutes. Real launches fly whatever the rocket switches say: the switches are for the random round (Dave's choice).
- **A rehearsal,** for testing and demos: `defaults write com.dtanquary.atrium spaceport.rehearse -int 240` plants a Falcon 9 (mission "Rehearsal") that many seconds out at the next poll, within 15 seconds while the switch is on, and clears the key. It stays in the list through the real fetches that follow until a minute after its T−0 (a side agent caught the first version losing it to the T−3 fetch, which scrubbed the rehearsal a minute in). - **A switch for each rocket** (`spaceport.rocket.falcon9`, `.heavy`, `.starship`, `.newglenn`, `.vulcan`, `.electron`, `.sls`, `.shuttle`, `.saturn`, `.ariane6`, `.ariane`, `.titan`, `.atlas`, `.redstone`, `.soyuz`, `.longmarch`, `.h3`, `.lvm3`, all on by default), in a Rockets section. ## Tuning constants
- **Starship's pad:** 69 columns left of the pad; its mount's top 13 rows above the shore; the tower 5 to 10 columns left of the rocket; the arms 43 rows above the booster's foot.
- **Spaceport:** water `0.235 × h` rows, the shore 3 rows above it; the pad 46% across, its deck 6 rows up and the launch mount 4 more; the ramp 30 columns long; the tower 58 rows with a 15-row mast; the hangar's door 105 columns from the mount; landing zones 77% and 89% across. Climb `0.65 t² (1 + t / 40)`; the lens true to 60 pixels up, then `60 + 90 ln(1 + (z − 60) / 90)`; the lean `0.0094 (z − 70)^1.8` columns before the lens. A booster falls from 330 at 34 px/s and burns from 170. The crane's hook hangs 33 columns left of it, 53 rows up.

## Performance
Release build, 2x, 1512×982:
- **The Spaceport,** measured 2026-10-05 with a Falcon 9: 0.54 ms CPU and 0.22 ms GPU through a count, 0.74 to 0.75 and 0.26 to 0.42 with the lift-off's steam at its fullest (dusk and night), 0.72 and 0.50 during the climb, 0.57 and 0.25 as a booster lands. The steam is the cost: about a thousand puffs added into the density field 15 times a second. The big rockets raise more of it and leave a thicker trail: SLS and the Saturn V measured 1.04 to 1.10 ms CPU and 0.22 to 0.25 ms GPU at lift-off and in the climb. Two other readings that day came out at 1.35 ms CPU (one with 0.88 ms GPU), with other sessions' tests running on the machine. Starship measured 0.82 to 0.88 ms CPU and 0.21 ms GPU at lift-off and in the climb.
- Inside the 2 ms budget, with the big rockets' lift-offs the dearest thing in either pixel wallpaper.
- **On the desktop it costs more than the test says.** `top` showed the app at 12 to 17% of a core during a launch, at 60 fps on mains power, where the test's 0.75 ms a frame would come to 4.5%. A wallpaper's frames come a sixtieth of a second apart, each one cold, on a core the system has clocked down, so the same code takes several times longer than in a test that runs frames back to back. What counts for the battery is each thing's share of a frame, and `sample` on the running app gives that.
- **Where a frame went** (`sample Atrium 270 5`, a whole round on the desktop, before the review below), as shares of the main thread's work on a frame:
  - 48% SpriteKit drawing and presenting the scene, which every wallpaper pays.
  - 12% the layer's texture: SpriteKit throws a mutable texture's Metal texture away and makes and uploads another each time the layer is repainted, which is 14 to 20 times a second averaged over a round.
  - 7% painting the layer's things, 8% painting the smoke.
  - 8% on two things nobody had looked at: the countdown board's text was formatted every frame to see whether it had changed (5%), and the Launches setting was read from UserDefaults three or four times a frame (3%).
- **The review (0.90.1),** at Dave's asking and with nothing to be seen of it (see his decisions):
  - **The board's text is made only when its second or its rocket changes.**
  - **The pace is read when the scene repaints** (`pace`, in `redraw`: every 30 seconds, and at once when a setting changes), not every frame. Reading it once a frame, down from three or four times, saved almost nothing: it is the first read of UserDefaults in a cold frame that costs, about 3% of the frame by itself.
  - **A scene with no traffic no longer tries to put a car on the road twice a frame.** With no cars to place, `spawnCar` never set the time to try again, so it was called for both lanes on every frame: 1 to 3% of one. That goes for any of Pixel City's cities without cars, too.
  - **`stamp` and `flame` look only at the rows and columns their turned rectangle can cover** (`span`), not at the whole square it could turn within. For a Falcon standing on the pad that was 11,449 pixels looked at to paint 230 at most; it's now about 500.
  - **The smoke clears only the rows its last painting reached** (`smoked`), where it cleared its whole canvas and the whole field of density each time. Each puff's columns are squared once, not once for every row, and the adding has no branch in it, so the compiler does several columns at a time. A row's three lights are written into one array, not into a new one for each row.
  - **A rocket that stands on a crawler has no strongback,** so the layer is no longer repainted as the lean it would have had changes through the climb.
  - **In a release build,** stepped through five minutes of one rocket at 60 frames a second, the scene's own code went from 0.035 ms a frame to 0.021 with a Falcon 9. Painting the smoke went from 0.113 ms a time to 0.072 (a Saturn V's from 0.106 to 0.064, Starship's from 0.119 to 0.081); painting the layer from 0.0100 ms a frame to 0.0058 (0.0091 to 0.0035, and 0.0124 to 0.0053); `update` from 0.0030 to 0.0017. Against the 0.54 to 0.75 ms the render test prints, that is too little to show there.
  - **On the desktop,** from the same `sample` over a round of the finished build (298), counting only the frames that were drawn and set against SpriteKit's drawing and presenting, which the review doesn't touch: the scene's own code does 49% less (`update` half, painting the smoke 60% less; the board's text, the setting's read, the traffic's check and `stamp` have all but left the profile), and a drawn frame costs the main thread about an eighth less. A profile part-way through said a sixth: the rounds flew different rockets, and the last made the layer's texture 16% more often. A drawn frame is now 58% SpriteKit drawing and presenting, 16% the layer's texture and 16% the scene's own code. `top` still reads 13 to 15% of a core during a launch: most of that was never the scene's to save.
  - **Not the scene's, and not looked into:** in that last profile the app's `view(_:shouldRenderAtTime:)` (main.swift), which sets every wallpaper's clock, and SpriteKit's own check of whether to draw took a fifth of the display link's time, against a twentieth in the first. A guess, not checked: the display was refreshing at 120 Hz for some of it, with the wallpaper drawing every other time.
  - **What is left is SpriteKit's:** drawing the frame, and the layer's texture. A mutable texture can't be given part of a picture. Cutting the layer into tiles would make more textures a second, not fewer, and making one is the dear part. Skipping an upload when the bytes come out the same as last time would save 4% of them with a Falcon and 11% with Starship, for a comparison of the whole layer on every repaint: not worth having.
  - **Not done, because it would show:** repainting the layer less often than things move (a falling booster moves every frame at 60 fps), and making the flames flicker in step with the smoke so the two share a repaint.

## Gotchas and shortcuts
- **The Spaceport paints at the end of a frame** (`didFinishUpdate`), not in `update`. In a debug build a frame with the lift-off's steam in it takes up to 18 ms (the steam adds about 0.2 ms in a release build), so a test that stepped the round through by `update` took 80 seconds for twenty minutes of launches. Now `update` only moves things on, and `pixelSpaceportKeepsLaunching` paints every twentieth step.
- **`Bytes`, not `Pixels`, for the Spaceport's layer:** premultiplied bytes in a mutable texture's own order, painted with plain floats. `Pixels` would convert 71,000 pixels of vectors for every repaint.
- **Puffs let off together must live about as long as each other.** With lifetimes spread from 40 to 70 seconds, the last of a trail to go hung in the sky as separate round dots, which read as bubbles.
- **The wide lens isn't real.** A launch seen from five kilometres stays in a wide shot for minutes and never gets small enough to matter; at the size these rockets are drawn it would leave the top of the picture in 14 seconds, still full size. The squeeze is there so the rocket can dwindle and its trail can arc.
- **A scene's first rocket has already rolled out,** so `pad.x` has to start at the full distance. It started at 0 until the second day's work, and the first strongback of every scene vanished at the end of the climb instead of rolling home. Nobody had noticed; it showed up as a visiting flag coming down too early.
- **A small cloud has to stay close in.** The small rockets' lift-off steam is a third of a Falcon's. Thrown out at the same speed it came apart into separate round puffs, like popcorn; it's now thrown a third as far.
- **The trail is laid back along the rocket's axis,** where its flame ends. The first build laid it straight below the rocket's foot, which is the same place only while the rocket is upright: once it leaned, the flame pointed down to the left and the smoke began below and to the right of it. Dave caught it the same day (see his decisions).
- **The first climb leaned too soon,** from 50 pixels up, and went across the top of the tower. It leans from 70.
- **Whatever replaces `smoke` or `density` has to empty `smoked` too.** The smoke clears only the rows its last painting reached, so a new canvas with an old range, or an old field with a new one, would leave stale cloud behind. `layOutSpaceport` drops all three together.
- **`span` keeps a pixel to spare each way, and never goes beyond the square the first build looked at.** The first is for rounding; the second is so that it can only ever look at fewer pixels than before, never different ones.
- **A switched-off fleet flies everything** (`ponytail:` in the source), so the pad is never left empty.
- **`K` is one list of names for both wallpapers' settings.** Pixel City's first three (`view`, `shuffle`, `shuffleMinutes`) are also read by position in `settingsChanged`, so they must stay first in `cityKnobs`.

Checked by `pixelSpaceportFollowsARealLaunch` (lift-off within 2 s of a planted T−0 by the schedule's clock, LIVE on the board), `launchScheduleReadsLaunchLibrary` and `launchSchedulePlansItsPolls`, and as frames every 2 seconds of a planted Falcon 9's live round beside a Saturn V's, and of one put on hold at T−15 and scrubbed at T+15 (HOLD, SCRUB, the lowering, the roll back lying).
  - **The floodlights at lift-off, 2026-10-07.** Dave: "one minor thing annoying me about rocket launches, is at night they are lit up by lights on the pad, but as soon as its t+1 and they start to lift off suddenly the lights just cut out, its odd, review that and let me know what you think, hopefully an easy fix". The pad's lamps never went out: the rocket was drawn fully floodlit on the pad and not at all in the air, so it went dark in one frame, and the steam near the pad lost the floodlights' tint at the same moment. Then "go ahead, fix it". The rocket now leaves the light as it climbs (above), and the cloud keeps it. Keeping the old light on the cloud showed its edge: a lift-off's cloud fills the circle it lit, and it came out a hard white disc, like a spotlight, in the cloud and its reflection; softened to its edge and capped at 60%, it reads as a glow. Checked as a Falcon 9's night lift-off at 21:30, a frame a second from T−4 to T+22, before, with the first try and with the soft glow.
  - **The floodlights at the catch, 2026-10-07.** Dave: "the bug we just fixed for the rocket launch lighting, we need to apply that same fix for the starship booster when it returns since its returning back to the lights". The same seam the other way: Super Heavy came down unlit and turned fully floodlit the frame the tower caught it. It now takes the floodlights over its last 80 rows, as a rocket leaves them. Checked as a Starship-only night round at 21:30, a frame a second over the last 20 seconds of the booster's fall and the catch, before and after.
  - **The landing zones' lights and the roll-out, 2026-10-07.** Dave: "and also the falcon 9 and falcon heavy returns need to have the proper glow on the smoke if not already there". The dust only had the flame's orange, so at night it went from orange to dead grey the moment the engine cut, and the booster stood unlit on its zone though lights ring it. The zone's lights now glow on the dust and light the booster, weaker than the pad's floodlights. A side agent's note found the same seam at the other end of the round: a rocket rolled out unlit and was floodlit the frame its raise began (and a scrubbed one went dark the frame it started home), and Starship's caught booster stayed fully lit riding home. All of these now go by the one rule, distance from the light. Checked as a Falcon 9's and a Starship's night rounds at 21:30, a frame a second (every two for the roll-out), before and after.
