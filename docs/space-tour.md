# Space Tour

A slow tour of the Sun's family and of deep space in the best photos spacecraft and telescopes have taken. Each view drifts and zooms for a minute (Ken Burns), then dissolves into the next. The tour moves from world to world, not photo to photo, so each visit to a world shows the next of its photos. The Sun and the Moon are live: today's Sun from SDO (The Sun Today's scene) and the Moon as it is right now where you are (The Moon's scene), run inside this one. Settings can keep the tour to the Solar System or to deep space, and hold it on one world or one kind of object.

It was two wallpapers, Solar System Tour and Deep Space Tour, until 2026-11-07 (see Merged, below). This doc covers the engine and the Solar System's photos; [deep-space.md](deep-space.md) covers deep space's.

- **Files:**
  - `Sources/Atrium/PhotoTour.swift`. `Tour` is a set of photos (its tables, what's been shown of it under a prefix, `solar` or `deep`, and the tour's choices, `pickNext` and `nextFacts`), with the tour's settings as statics; `Tour.next` picks between the sets. `PhotoTour` is the scene (the framing and cut, `cut`, and the shader).
  - `Sources/Atrium/Resources/solar-facts.tsv`: facts for the caption's third line, a few per world (`world<TAB>fact`), from NASA's fact sheets.
  - `Sources/Atrium/Resources/solar-photos.tsv`: one row per view, from the Sun outward: file, world, credit, licence, source, caption, kind (`disc`, `closeup` or `live`) and focus. About reads its credits.
  - `Sources/Atrium/Resources/solar-<name>.heic`: the photos, cut by `docs/solar-system/prep.py`. `solar-thumb-<world>.jpg`: a 152×92 swatch per world for Settings.
  - `docs/solar-system/photos.py`: every photo's original, crop, focus, caption and credit, the source of truth for the TSV. `docs/solar-system/prep.py` cuts them (see Photos below).
  - `Tests/AtriumTests/SpaceTourTests.swift`: the caption's place; `RenderTests.swift` has the tour's choices (`spaceTourKeepsToShow`, `tourHeldOnGroundOnlyWorldShowsIt`).
- **Entry:** `spaceTour(size:)`. Its registry entry is "Space Tour", icon `smallcircle.filled.circle`, tint `.orange`, knobs `Tour.knobs`, then The Sun's (with `TheSun.wavelengthKnob`) and The Moon's, each moved under its own section with `Knob.in(_:)`, which also hides them while the tour is kept to deep space, and looks `Tour.solarSystem.showChoice(at: 1)` and `Tour.deepSpace.showChoice(at: 2)`: the worlds and the kinds of object, each with its thumbnail, under the Show menu.
- **Kind:** photos in one full-screen SKShader, and two live scenes nested in this one.

## How it works
- **A view** is a row of the TSV. `disc`: a whole world (or a crescent) on black. `closeup`: a surface or cloud tops seen from orbit, filling the screen. `surface`: a view from the ground (rover panoramas, Apollo, the last seconds before touching down), framed like a close-up and left out by the Views from the surface switch. `live`: `TheSun` or `TheMoon`. A close-up or surface photo too small to fill the screen without magnifying past 1.5× (Huygens on Titan, NEAR over Eros) is shown whole on black instead, its edges faded to black over 6% of its short side (`wholeEdge`), so it never shows as a lighter square.
- **Which set** (`Tour.next`): the one Show is at, or with Everything, the Solar System or deep space at even odds, then a body within it as below. By count of bodies deep space would come up a little more (71 to 44); half and half keeps either from drowning the other out (Dave, 2026-11-07). Each set keeps its own rotation, under its own prefix.
- **The tour** (`pickNext`): the world held in Settings, or else a random world not among the last half of all worlds shown (`solar.recent`), weighted by how many views it has up to 3, so Saturn and Jupiter come round more often than Umbriel but not twelve times as often. Then that world's next view in the TSV's order (`solar.seen`, a count per world), so a return to Saturn shows a different Saturn. Both are kept in UserDefaults, so the rotation carries on across launches. Two live views never follow each other (see Gotchas). Each display and the Settings preview tour on their own, sharing the counts.
- **Timing:** each view shows for `solar.seconds` (60 s), then the next dissolves in over 6 s with a smoothstep. A view's motion runs over its time plus its dissolve out, so nothing stops moving on screen. The next photo is cut in the background once the current one has fully arrived; if it isn't ready, the current view holds at the end of its motion.
- **The menu bar's still:** as each dissolve ends the scene posts `.pictureChanged`, and with Match the lock screen on the app shows macOS a small still of the new photo (256 px across) for four seconds, then gives it the lock screen's own still back. macOS picks the menu bar's text colour from the small one, black once the strip under the bar averages about 155 of 255, so the bar suits the photo that is up, and the lock screen keeps its sharp still, taken every hour as for any wallpaper: see The menu bar, below.
- **Framing** (`cut`), in photo pixels and points:
  - **Fill** (Framing, the default since 2026-09-28; Dave: "we should default all images to 'cover' mode, there are times when a fantastic image pops up but has black bars on the sides", for both tours): every photo, whole worlds included, covers the screen and pans toward its focus, as a close-up does, unless covering would magnify it past 1.5× (Umbriel, Callisto's narrow crescent, Huygens on Titan): those are shown whole. A whole world stays 5% in from its edges, its black margin or a deep-sky photo's baked-in edge fade, so the screen's edges never darken. With Show the whole photo, whole worlds are framed as below.
  - **Whole worlds** fit 86% of the screen's height or 94% of its width at their widest, and zoom by `solar.zoom` (1.25×) or the row's own zoom. The zoom heads halfway to the focus, so the world stays whole and near the middle; the wide end is jittered by up to 4% of the screen.
  - **Never soft:** the tight end is capped at 1.5 screen pixels per photo pixel. A small photo (Voyager's Umbriel, 280 px) shows smaller on black instead, as Dave chose.
  - **Close-ups** cover the screen at their widest and zoom toward the focus, clamped so the screen never leaves the photo. They start from the focus's reflection through the middle, so a long strip (Juno's limbs, Pluto at sunset) pans across it, but never more than 0.6 of a screen's width in one view, so it stays slow. A photo more than 2.2 screens wide (a rover panorama) aims at a random stretch each visit instead of its focus.
  - **Direction:** half zoom in, half out (`Bool.random()`).
- **The cut:** only the part of the photo the motion ever shows (the union of its two ends' views, which contain every frame between: the left edge is concave in time and the right convex), decoded off the main thread, scaled with Core Graphics' high interpolation so the widest moment is one texel per screen pixel (or the photo's own pixels, if fewer). After that the texture only ever magnifies, so fine detail like ringlets never aliases or shimmers.
- **Shader:** both views (showing, and dissolving in) are sampled with Catmull-Rom in nine bilinear taps (Sigg and Hadwiger's trick), which stays sharp as it slowly magnifies where bilinear blurs, and pulses as the photo slides across texels. Outside a photo is black. A live view's slot is clear, and the sprite blends with `.alpha` (premultiplied), so the live scene below shows through; the dissolve between a photo and a live view is the photo's alpha. A close-up or ground view shown whole fades out toward its edges (`edgeFade`, over the last `u_pa.w` texels, the same smoothstep as prep.py's `feather`); it's cut whole for that, so the fade runs along its own edges. ±½/255 dither.
- **Live views:** `liveScene` builds `TheSun` or `TheMoon`, adds it as a child below the photo sprite, and passes it `update(_:)` each frame and `didMove(to:)`. SpriteKit runs actions on a nested scene and draws it (checked on 2026-09-27), but never calls those two itself. A live view that's next is built hidden when chosen, so its images load behind the current photo, and removed once the next view has dissolved in.
- **Glare** (`glare`, Soften bright photos): the cut also draws itself 32 pixels square and takes its mean level (Rec. 709 luma of the sRGB values). Up to 110 of 255, as most photos are, it's shown as it is; above, it's dimmed by (110 / mean)^0.7 (`u_gain`, one per slot, on the colour and not the alpha, so a dissolve still goes from one to the other). The review of 2026-11-14 measured the photos' means at 4 to 176 of 255, so a minute of near-white ground could follow a crescent on black, and the Brightness slider, one value for every photo, can only dim M87* with Mount Hadley. Measured on the cuts at their tight ends: Apollo 15's Mount Hadley comes down to 0.67 of itself, Juno's Great Red Spot 0.77, Mars's defrosting dunes 0.78, Earth's glint 0.81, Jezero 0.83; a photo on black isn't touched, and nor is the caption. Dave's rule from Zen Garden: calm means soft and dim. Free: the mean costs about a tenth of a millisecond in the background cut, and the shader one multiply.
- **Captions** (see Caption in Settings): the world's name (SF Pro 26 pt semibold, 82% white) over one line (13 pt, 60%): what, which spacecraft, when, and the colour if it isn't true colour. Under them, dimmer (12 pt, 45%), facts about the world (see Caption in Settings). Lines stack by size: each baseline is 0.3 × the last line's size plus 1.3 × its own below the last. They fade in 1.5 s after a view arrives, over 3 s, and out as the next begins. The Moon's line names the picked phase when it isn't real time.
- **The caption's shadow** (`layoutCaption`, 2026-11-16): the lines are painted with Core Text once a view into a sprite at 2x, over a second sprite of their own soft shadow: each line drawn nine times a little apart (15% of its size round it), off the canvas so only its shadow lands, black at 55% blurred 9 pt (times Size), painted at half a pixel a point since it's soft. Over black it's invisible; over a sunlit photo it darkens the ground just round the words, so even the 45% facts read. The review of 2026-11-14 found the second and third lines all but gone over Apollo 16's ground and the third lost among the Pillars' stars, and the shader's old shadow (a Gaussian darkening of up to 45% reaching 100 × 65 pt past the block) showing as a dark smudge on the Moon's Sky backdrop. SKLabelNode does draw an NSShadow, but scaled by the text's own alpha, so the dim lines got almost none; so the shader's shadow is gone and the caption is painted. It's placed on whole pixels so the text stays as sharp as SKLabelNode's. Painting takes about 2.5 ms once a view on the main thread (release; 10 ms for the first after launch, 6 ms at Size 2×), a tenth of what a thickened outline at full resolution took.

## Facts
- **What:** 231 short facts (`solar-facts.tsv`), 3 to 10 per world, more for worlds with more photos (Jupiter, Saturn and Mars have 10), so a world you keep coming back to keeps saying something new. Sizes, days, years, tilts, temperatures and orbits are from NASA's planetary and satellite fact sheets (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet, updated March 2025); the rest from NASA mission pages. Distances in km, temperatures in °C. Moon counts were left out: they change as moons are found (Uranus gained one in 2025).
- **Rotation** (`nextFacts`): each world's facts in a shuffled order that's the same every launch (a `SplitMix` seeded by an FNV-1a hash of the name; Swift's `hashValue` changes per launch), two per visit, continuing from `solar.factsSeen` (a count per world). None repeats until all of that world's have shown.

## Settings
- **Show:** Tour (`solar.tour`: Everything, the default; The Solar System; Deep space), a menu, with a grid of thumbnails under it at either set: the worlds (`solar.body`, by name; empty is Random) or deep space's kinds of object (`deep.show`). Random tours the whole set; a world holds the tour there, still moving through its views. At Everything there's no grid and neither hold counts. A change of either dissolves straight to a view from the new pick once it's cut (`moveOn` in the scene: the view that was next, and any cut under way, are dropped), unless a dissolve is already under way; nothing is rebuilt.
- **Settings are shared by both sets** and keep Solar System Tour's `solar.` keys, so nothing moved for anyone who was on it.
- **Caption:** Show (`solar.captionLines`: nothing, the name, the name and a line on the photo, or those and facts; the last by default), Facts (`solar.factCount`: one, two or three at a time; two), Facts laid out (`solar.factLayout`: on one line, joined by a dot, or one per line), Position (`solar.captionPlace`: a top or bottom corner or the middle of either edge; bottom left), Size (`solar.captionSize`, 0.6–2×), Margin (`solar.captionMargin`, 16–240 pt from the edges; 52). A change lays the caption out again at once, keeping its facts. Right-hand positions right-align the lines and the middle ones centre them. The caption's shadow is painted with it, so it follows wherever the block lands. Names and Facts were two switches until 2026-09-28; main.swift moves an old setting across.
- **Tour:** Each view for (`solar.seconds`, 20–300 s, 60), Zoom (`solar.zoom`, 1–1.6×, 1.25), Views from the surface (`solar.surface`, hidden at Deep space; see below), Framing (`solar.framing`: Fill the screen, the default, or Show the whole photo; see Framing), Brightness (`solar.brightness`, 0.4–1.2×, 1; photos only, the live views have their own), Soften bright photos (`solar.soften`, on; see Glare).
- **The Sun:** Wavelength (`sun.light`, Random or one of the eight), then the rest of the Sun's settings (see [sun.md](sun.md)). Show held the palette slot, so the wavelength became a menu.
- **The Moon:** the Moon's settings (see [the-moon.md](the-moon.md)).
- **For trying things out** (`defaults write com.dtanquary.atrium …`): `solar.photo` or `deep.photo` pins one view by file name (`live-the-sun`, `live-the-moon` for the live views), whatever Show is at; `solar.at` (0–1) holds the motion at that point, for snapshots.

## Photos
- **What's in:** whole worlds and close-ups from orbit, the best-looking version of each, true or enhanced colour, infrared or radar, with the caption saying which (Dave's picks, 2026-09-27). No surface landscapes, famous-moment shots (Earthrise, Pale Blue Dot), renders, or spacecraft in frame.
- **Research,** 2026-09-27: five agents, one per region (Sun to Moon; Mars and the small worlds; Jupiter; Saturn; Uranus outward), searched NASA Photojournal (now at science.nasa.gov, full-size files under `assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/psd/photojournal/pia/piaNN/piaNNNNN/`), images.nasa.gov, ESA, ESA/Webb and ESA/Hubble, JAXA, pluto.jhuapl.edu and Wikimedia Commons and Flickr (for citizen processing), downloaded the originals, and checked each one at 1:1.
- **Licences:** public domain, CC BY or CC BY-SA only (ESA's CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO, Webb's and Hubble's CC BY 4.0), credited in About from the TSV. Out: anything non-commercial or unclear. Juno images processed by volunteers are often NC (Seán Doran, Gerald Eichstädt, Björn Jónsson) or published by NASA with no licence (PIA21641's south pole, PIA26484's Io), so only those marked CC BY are in. Callisto (Justin Cowart's Voyager 2 mosaic) was CC BY 2.0 when Wikimedia Commons' licence review checked it; its Flickr page now says CC BY-NC, but a CC licence can't be withdrawn from a copy already given under it.
- **Cutting** (`prep.py`, with numpy, Pillow, scipy and ffmpeg):
  - **Load** as floats, colour-managed to sRGB; 16-bit TIFFs Pillow can't read go through ffmpeg as rgb48.
  - **Crop** (`crop`, fractions of the original): labels, credit text, logos, projection edges.
  - **Despeckle** (`despeckle`): JunoCam frames carry a grid of dark marks every 118 px; pixels more than t darker than their 5-pixel median (t = 0.12) are replaced by the 7-pixel median. Dark only: both ways caught 6% of pixels, real cloud texture.
  - **Black level,** whole worlds only: the black point is the border's median plus 3σ of its noise (exact zeros, a padded frame, left out), and a soft toe, y²/(y + 0.015), keeps faint haze and night sides instead of clipping them. Every photo then sits on the same black, so the dissolves don't pump.
  - **Box,** whole worlds only: the biggest bright blob (so stars and small moons don't widen it), or `box` for a crescent or a world with its moon, plus 7% margin, padded with black.
  - **Fade** (`feather`, a fraction of the short side): a whole world cut off by its original's frame (the 2012 eruption, Artemis II's Orientale, Pluto's haze ring, 67P) fades out there instead of ending in a straight line inside the black margin. It runs after the black level. With `keepblack` and a full `box`, it also floats a photo with no black around it (Titan before the rings, Daphnis in the Keeler gap, 67P's cliffs) on black.
  - **Saturation** (`saturation`): the research agent's 8-bit conversions of Mars Express's global mosaic came out much redder than Viking's globes; 0.7 brings them into the same butterscotch.
  - **Vignette** (`vignette`, the fraction of the radius that fades): an oval fade for a photo whose whole background is lit (Titan before Saturn, Daphnis in the ring plane, 67P's cliffs). The rectangular `feather` left a glowing rectangle floating on black; an oval reads as a soft glow.
  - **Size:** whole worlds at most 3200 px (`cap`; 4000–4800 for the Sun and Pluto, which zoom deeper), close-ups 5120 px, long strips keep a 3400 px short side. `shrink` takes an upscaled original back toward its real sharpness (Andrea Luck's Neptune close-up 0.6, Triton 0.7, Arrokoth and Nix 0.6).
  - **Save:** HEIC at quality 80 via `sips`, with an sRGB profile and ±½ dither; clean at 1:1. Whole worlds are mostly black, so many are under 0.2 MB.
  - **Focus** (`at`) is given in the original's fractions, as the research agents reported it, and moved through the crop, box and scaling.
  - **Thumbnails:** each world's first photo (or the one marked `thumb`): whole worlds 62% of the box around the middle, close-ups from their middle.
- **The order** within a world is the order the tour shows them: the best first.

## Views from the surface
- **What** (Dave, 2026-09-28: "lets also discuss the idea of opening up to non earth surface images"; then: mixed in with a switch, astronauts and hardware allowed): 14 rover and helicopter photos of Mars (Curiosity, Perseverance, Spirit, Opportunity, Ingenuity), 13 Apollo photos of the Moon (six NASA JSC panoramas and seven single Hasselblad frames from the Project Apollo Archive), Huygens's one photo from Titan's surface, Rosetta's last-day descent on comet 67P, Hayabusa2's touchdowns on Ryugu, OSIRIS-REx grabbing Bennu, DART's last seconds before Dimorphos and NEAR landing on Eros. Dimorphos and Eros are new worlds that only have views from the ground.
- **Woven:** each world's views from orbit and from the ground alternate in the table (`weave` in photos.py), so a visit to Mars goes orbit, ground, orbit.
- **Switch** (`solar.surface`, Views from the surface, on): hidden while Show is at Deep space, since every view from the ground is the Solar System's; off, the tour skips them and the worlds that only have them, unless Show is held on one of those (Eros, Dimorphos), which then shows its views from the ground rather than nothing (it went black, found in the 1.0 review, 2026-10-08).
- **Panoramas** (`split` in photos.py): a panorama many screens long is cut into 2 or 3 overlapping pieces, each a view of its own, so none is huge to decode and each visit pans a stretch of it. The prep crops before converting to floats, so a 30,000-pixel panorama never becomes gigabytes.
- **Colour:** Perseverance's best panoramas were only published in enhanced colour (its natural-colour versions' links are dead), and Curiosity's are mostly adjusted to Earth-like lighting; the captions say so.
- **Left out:** Venus: the only surface photos (Venera 9, 10, 13 and 14) aren't clearly public domain (NASA's NSSDCA says "not necessarily in the public domain") and the well-known reprocessings are copyrighted. Philae's panorama and ROLIS images are ESA Standard Licence, and ASU's high-resolution Apollo scans are non-commercial. Viking's lander images top out at 2497×512 px. Chinese and commercial lunar landers' licences aren't CC or public domain. Itokawa's frames couldn't be checked for mirroring.
- **Size:** capped at 4096 px, strips at 2600 px tall, HEIC at 60 (Dave chose this over 5120 px to keep the app near 330 MB).

## Performance
- 0.49–0.55 ms CPU and 0.26–0.32 ms GPU for a photo, 0.85 ms GPU with the live Sun (release, 2x Retina at 1512×982, 2026-09-27). A dissolve samples two photos, still well under a millisecond.
- **Memory:** two textures at up to screen size (24 MB each at 3024×1964), plus a transient full decode while cutting (up to 70 MB, in the background).
- **Cutting** takes 110–240 ms per photo. Only the first view is cut on the main thread, when the scene is built, so the render test has a photo.
- **App size:** the Solar System's photos are about 190 MB with the views from the surface (2026-09-28).
- **On battery it runs at 20 fps at most** (2026-10-13), and less over a live view (2026-11-16): the scene sets its own rate (`BatteryPaced`, `rest()` each frame), so the entry in Scenes.swift has none. Its pans are the same for both sets: up to about 34 device pixels (2x on a 14-inch MacBook Pro, 3024 × 1964) a second, 1.7 px a frame at 20 fps, and 4.4 with Each view for at its shortest, so 20 holds while a photo shows, through every dissolve, and while the caption fades in or out. With a live view alone on screen and its caption in, it's 15 for the Sun (its plasma moves about 7 px a second and its eruptions up to 17, a pixel a frame) and 10 for the Moon, which is lit again once a minute and turns with the sky by a few degrees an hour; Earth from Orbit rests at 10 the same way. Held on the Moon, where main.swift moved everyone who had The Moon as a wallpaper, that halves the frames on battery.
- **The photo layer over a live view** is hidden while no dissolve runs: it was clear there, but its nine-read shader still ran full-screen on a 1 × 1 black texture. With the live Moon that took 0.25–0.35 ms GPU a frame down to 0.12–0.22 (debug, back to back, under load), and the picture is the same to the byte (checked at a picked phase, which doesn't turn with the clock). The caption's shadow, painted with it since the same day, needs nothing of the photo layer.

## Gotchas and shortcuts
- **SpriteKit leaves a texture uniform undeclared while its texture is nil,** and the shader then fails to compile. Both slots start with a 1-pixel black texture.
- **A child's zPosition adds to its parent's,** so a nested live scene's own layers (The Moon's sky at 2, its disc at 1) drew over the photo layer at 1: the live Moon popped in and out instead of dissolving (Dave, 2026-09-27: "the 1st image did not fade out it popped out"). The photo layer is at 100 and the caption at 101, above anything the live scenes use.
- **Nested scenes ignore alpha** in their own shaders, so a live view can't be faded. The photo layer sits above it and fades instead. Two live views would both show through at once, which is why the tour never puts them back to back.
- **Render tests** never finish a background cut (the detached task needs the main actor the test holds), so they only ever show the first view; use `solar.photo` and `solar.at` to see a given photo at a given point.
- `ponytail:` every display and the Settings preview cut their own copy of a photo. Share cuts across copies if memory on many displays matters.
- `ponytail:` the first cut blocks the main thread for up to 240 ms. Start on black and cut it in the background if the switch to Space Tour feels sticky.

## Edges on black
- 2026-11-07, Dave: "bug in Dimorphos, its edges dont fade all the way to black so on solid black fading bg you can see the outline and it doesnt blend well". DART's frame from 68 km (`solar-dimorphos-whole`) is 1041 px, so it's always shown whole on black, and its background is a dark grey (7 of 255 round its edges, up to 17), where its sister frame and Eros's had a `vignette` baked in by prep.py and it had none. Every photo shown whole was measured: on a 14-inch MacBook Pro it was the only one with a lit edge, but on a 5K screen about twenty close-ups can't fill the screen without going soft (Juno's Jupiter, Rosetta's 67P, Webb's Pillars) and showed as hard-edged rectangles on black. So the shader fades the edges of every close-up or ground view shown whole (a whole world already sits in its own black margin). That alone left Dimorphos a soft dark-grey square: its background is scattered light round the moonlet, brightest toward the frame's edges, with a band of brighter rows along the bottom. So it was re-cut with its sister frame's `vignette=0.3` too, from NASA's original (`all_dimorphos_dart_0401930040_12262_01_iof_imagedisplay-final.png` on the source page; `SOLAR_ORIGINALS=… python3 -E -P prep.py dimorphos-whole`, since `-I` hides scipy in the user site-packages): its frame's edges are now 0.3 of 255 on average, a round glow on black. The oval takes the sliver of Didymos's limb in the top-left corner with it, as it does in the sister frame. The fade costs nothing measurable (0.22 to 0.25 ms GPU a frame either way).

## The menu bar
- 2026-11-13, Dave: "once in a while, seemingly at random, the top bar will shift to like 'light mode' where its black text with a subtle white gradient under it when it has no reason to", with Europa on black under it.
- **What macOS does** (27.2.1, found on his Mac with the wallpaper still running): the window server works out the bar's colour from the strip of system wallpaper under it, but only while some of that wallpaper reaches the screen, and once more five seconds after each unlock. With Match the lock screen on the system wallpaper is Atrium's still, and Atrium's window hides all of it, so the bar kept whatever the still at the last unlock gave it for the rest of the session, through every photo after. Checked: with Atrium running, neither a flat white or black system wallpaper nor a white strip drawn at the desktop's own level moved the bar, and nor did the display sleeping and waking without a lock; quitting Atrium moved it within a tenth of a second; a flat white still, a lock and an unlock turned it black on light over a dark photo at will, and a flat black one turned it back. The menu bar agent logs what it's told, at debug level only: `log stream --level debug --predicate 'process == "MenuBarAgent" AND eventMessage CONTAINS "Received appearance"'`.
- **What lets it through:** a stand-in window over the desktop, in nine ways. Opaque, or marked not opaque with every pixel opaque: frozen. One clear pixel anywhere in the window, or the whole window at 99.9% or 99%: the bar followed the system wallpaper under it within a second. So `LockScreen.reread` holds the wallpaper windows at 99.9% for four seconds after every still set, and the tour asks for one per photo (a small one, since the 14th: below).
- **Checked on the desktop:** the tour held on Mars at 20 s a photo, eleven photos: the bar followed each still within a second, black text at 161 and 175 under the bar, white at 151 and below.
- 2026-11-14, Dave, of the still a photo: "is that a cache constantly over written? or is that ever growing? we cannot just forever be growing that".
- **What macOS keeps:** Atrium's own folder holds one still a display, the last deleted as the next is set. But macOS's image extension writes every still it is given into a cache of its own, as an uncompressed bitmap at the still's size: `<hash>-3024-1964-0-<the file's date>.bmp` in `~/Library/Containers/com.apple.wallpaper.agent/Data/Library/Caches/com.apple.wallpaper.caches/extension-com.apple.wallpaper.extension.image/`, which only macOS can read. Its log names each one (`process == "WallpaperImageExtension" AND category == "image-cache"`), and the bytes it has written are in `proc_pid_rusage`: 344 stills and 6,132 MB since the Mac was restarted two days before, 17.8 MB each, which is 3024 × 1964 × 3. Nothing was seen to clear it: a file made again with the name and date of a still from 10 minutes, 2, 11, 19, 36 and 42 hours before (the oldest the log gave a name for) was found in the cache each time, with nothing written. The code beside it has a `com.apple.wallpaper.cache-delete` and marks files purgeable, which is how macOS clears a cache when the disk runs short, but ten new stills took 178 MB off the disk's free space, counted either way, and no purge showed in two days of log. So every still for the lock screen costs 17.8 MB that Atrium can't take back: about six an hour for any wallpaper, since Match the lock screen began, and sixty an hour on the tour for the one night it took a still a photo (5 GB).
- **A small still for the bar:** the bitmap macOS keeps is the size of the picture it is given (a 378 × 246 one: 0.3 MB), so the still taken as a photo comes in is 256 px across, 0.13 MB kept, and four seconds later the lock screen's own still is set again, which macOS still has in hand and writes nothing for. The same is done six seconds after an unlock, for any wallpaper, since macOS dresses the bar again five seconds after one, by the lock screen's still. Checked on the desktop, the tour on Mars at 20 s a photo: a photo at 191 under the bar turned it black on light within a second, it stayed so once the lock screen's still (66) was back, and the next photo (95) turned it white; twenty photos, 2.6 MB written where a still each would have been 356 MB.
- **The lock screen's still, hourly:** told that the ten-minute still for the lock screen was still adding 17.8 MB each time, about 100 MB for each hour a wallpaper is in view, and that taking it hourly with a small one for the bar every ten minutes would make that about 18 MB, at the price of a lock screen up to an hour behind the wallpaper when the screen is locked by hand, Dave: "implement your recommendation". So for every wallpaper the ten-minute timer takes the small still, and the lock screen's own once that is 55 minutes old; a switch of wallpaper and the displays sleeping take the lock screen's own as before. It still grows, by a sixth of what it did: only Match the lock screen turned off adds nothing.
- **Not checked:** a second display, a Space other than the one in front, and the small still after an unlock (it takes Dave's password). Whether macOS clears that cache at a restart, or ever.
